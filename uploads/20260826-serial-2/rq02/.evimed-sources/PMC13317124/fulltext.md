# Temporal trends and prognostic impact of pre-hospital delay in ST-elevation myocardial infarction—20-year observational study from the SWEDEHEART registry

- PMCID: PMC13317124
- DOI: 10.1093/ehjacc/zuaf165
- Primary source: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC13317124
- Retrieved: 2026-08-26T07:23:12.232416Z

## Abstract

Abstract Aims Longer ischaemic time during ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is associated with higher mortality. However, how prehospital delay time affects outcomes and has evolved over time remains unknown. The aims were to study prognostic impact of prehospital delay time in STEMI patients during two decades in Sweden and to explore temporal trends and differences stratified by age, sex, and presence of diabetes mellitus. Methods and results Observational prospective cohort study based on the SWEDEHEART registry 1998–2017. Associations between prehospital delay time and 14 day, 1 year, and 5 year mortality were estimated with Kaplan Meier and Cox regression analyses. Among 89 155 patients higher short- and long-term mortality was observed with increasing prehospital delay time. The multivariable adjusted risk of 14 day mortality increased approximately 2% per hour of delay [hazard ratio (HR) 1.018, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.011–1.025], whereas the corresponding number for 1 and 5 year mortality was 1% (HR 1.011, 95% CI 1.006–1.016 and HR 1.009, 95% CI 1.005–1.013, respectively). No significant trend of prehospital delay time during the 20 years was observed [median 150 min, interquartile range (IQR) 80–302], as the curve was hump-shaped. During the fibrinolytic era (1998–2004), prehospital delay time significantly increased, while it decreased during the primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) era (2005–17). Consistently prolonged prehospital delay times were shown in women, elderly (≥70 years), and patients with diabetes with a delay 25–30 min longer than their counterparts. Conclusion Prehospital delay is an independent predictor of mortality. Whereas no declining trend was observed the last 20 years, it is reassuring that a decrease of delay was found during the PPCI era. Women, elderly, and individuals with diabetes had consistently prolonged delay times and needs to be targeted in future interventions. Keywords: Myocardial infarction, Acute coronary syndrome, Survival, Cohort study, Ischaemic time, Prehospital delay time

## Introduction

Despite improvements in prevention, the incidence of myocardial infarction (MI) and its associated mortality and morbidity remains high, with an immense impact on patients and healthcare systems. 1 Fast resolving of the coronary thrombus is the key treatment of ST-elevation MI (STEMI), with primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) within 90 min as first line therapy. 2 A prolonged prehospital delay time (PHDT) is detrimental since it increases total ischaemic time and myocardial damage with its complications such as left ventricular (LV) dysfunction and lethal arrhythmias. 3–5 An immediate decision and action by the patient at symptom onset, calling an ambulance as first medical contact (FMC), is vital to diminish PHDT. 6 Factors influencing PHDT are multifactorial; societal, such as national healthcare strategies 7 ; individual such as living conditions, age, 8 sex, 9 and comorbidities; 10 as well as contextual, such as bystander presence, time, and place of symptom onset. 6 In addition, triage of STEMI patients requires the presence of well-trained healthcare providers, with an integrated emergency medical service (EMS) in an organized network. 1 , 11 , 12 Studies during the fibrinolytic era have consistently found prolonged PHDT without substantial improvement over time. 13–17 However, contemporary studies are scarce and have mostly restricted their inclusion to PPCI populations. 18–22 With advancements in STEMI care, with the diagnosis made prehospitally, we hypothesize that PHDT in STEMI has shortened the last two decades. We also hypothesize that PHDT is independently associated with short- and long-term mortality. Therefore, we aimed to explore 20 year temporal trends of PHDT in STEMI all-comers, in total as well as stratified by age, sex, and presence of diabetes mellitus and assessing its prognostic impact.

### Study objectives

The first objective was to assess the association between PHDT and 14 day, 1 year, and 5 year mortality. The second objective was to assess PHDT trends over two decades in total as well as stratified by age, sex, and presence of diabetes mellitus. PHDT trends were aimed to be studied during the 20 year period as well as in the fibrinolytic and PPCI eras, separately.

## Methods

### Design and data sources

This was an observational cohort study based on prospectively collected data from the Swedish web-system for enhancement and development of evidence-based care in heart disease evaluated according to recommended therapies (SWEDEHEART). 23 The registry has national coverage with all 72 Swedish hospitals participating whereof 28 having PPCI facilities and almost all hospitalized MI patients (>90%) are included. 24 The registry contains information on transportation mode, clinical status on admission, key time-points, time of symptom onset, diagnostic ECG, medical history, medications on admission, during hospital care and at discharge, in-hospital procedures such as coronary angiography, PPCI and echocardiography, complications, outcome, and planned follow-up. SWEDEHEART is regularly monitored with more than 95% agreement towards medical records. 24 In this study, the National Board of Health and Welfare merged SWEDEHEART data with data from the Swedish National Patient Registry, including information on comorbidities; the Swedish National Population Registry, including information on vital status; and the Swedish Cause of Death Registry, using the unique Swedish personal identification number.

### Study population

Inclusion criteria for the present study were as follows: (i) registered in the SWEDEHEART registry between 1 January 1998 and 31 December 2017 with a confirmed diagnosis of STEMI; (ii) valid timepoint data for symptom onset, and (iii) first documented STEMI event in the registry. For the survival analyses, patients without valid information on vital status during follow-up and/or treatment with prehospital fibrinolysis were excluded ( Figure 1 ).

### Definition of PHDT and the fibrinolytic and PPCI eras

PHDT was defined as symptom onset until hospital admission, specified as the emergency room (ER), the coronary care unit (CCU), or the catheterization laboratory (cath lab). In this study 1998–2004 will be referred to as the fibrinolytic era, whereas 2005–17 will be referred to as the PPCI era. This cut-off was chosen as the shift of the predominant reperfusion strategy occurred in Sweden in 2004–05.

### Ethics

Patients are informed about the SWEDEHEART registry during hospitalization and can decline participation. Written consent is not required according to Swedish law but very few patients chose to opt out participation. The study protocol was approved by the regional ethics committee in Stockholm (D-nr: 2012/60–31/2; 2015/215-32; 2017/432-32).

### Statistical analysis

Four time periods (1998–2002, 2003–07, 2008–12 and 2013–17), as well as six prehospital delay groups (≤ 1 h, >1–2 h, >2–3 h, >3–6 h, >6–12 h, and >12 h), were compared as regards baseline characteristics, diagnostics, treatment, complications, and mortality. Categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages whereas continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles [presented as interquartile range (IQR)]. Differences in continuous variables were assessed using the ANOVA test for variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on if the variable was normally distributed or not. Differences in categorical variables were assessed using the χ 2 test.

Cumulative incidence of mortality within 14 days, 1 year, and 5 years was estimated and presented with the Kaplan–Meier method. Kaplan–Meier curves were stratified by prehospital delay with the six PHDT groups and statistical significance was tested with the log-rank test. As a sensitivity analyses, these analyses were performed after exclusion of patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and cardiogenic shock (CS). To assess the effect of PHDT on mortality after adjusting for possible confounders (for covariate details, see below) previously known to be associated with delay as well as with mortality, Cox proportion hazard regression analyses were performed. The outcomes were 14 day, 1 year, and 5 year mortality, in all patients as well as in-hospital survivors. In addition, these analyses were performed stratifying for treatment with PPCI. These models explored the association between PHDT as a continuous variable per hour of delay until a maximum of 12 h, adjusting stepwise for plausible confounders; Model 1, crude model only including PHDT per hour of increase; Model 2, adding age per year of increase; and Model 3, further adding sex, baseline characteristics [year of inclusion in 5 year blocks, diabetes, hypertension, previous MI, previous stroke, chronic heart failure (CHF), previous revascularization, out of hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and any given reperfusion therapy]. In addition, the same analyses, Models 1–3, were performed in-hospital survivors, with addition of discharge medication added to Model 3 [aspirin, P 2 Y 12 -receptor blockers, beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and statins]. Results from the Cox proportion hazard regression analyses are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated for the main exposure variable using visual inspection of Kaplan–Meier curves.

Median PHDT trends are presented for the whole cohort, as well as stratified according to sex, age < or ≥70 years, and the presence or absence of diabetes. To test PHDT differences during the 20 year period, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, while the Jonckheere–Terpstra test was used testing for trends. These tests were additionally performed in the fibrinolytic and the PPCI eras, separately. As a supplementary analysis, variables associated with prolonged PHDT in univariate analyses were assessed in a multivariable analysis. This was performed as linear regression with PHDT, log-transformed, as the dependant variable and sex, age, year of inclusion, ambulance transportation to hospital, chest pain at onset, diabetes, hypertension, previous MI, previous revascularization, and previous stroke as independent variables. The results are presented as beta-coefficients and P -values.

A P -value of 0.05 was set to indicate statistical significance in all analyses. Imputation was not performed, as the percentage of missing values was overall low ( Tables 1 and 2 as well as Supplementary material online , Tables S1 and S2 ). All analyses were conducted using the SPSS statistical programme (SPSS, version 28) software from SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL.

## Results

### Characteristics of the study population

The study population consisted of 89 155 STEMI patients with a mean age of 68.4 years (SD 12.5), whereof 67.4% (n = 60 132) were men, 15.9% had suffered from previous MI, 6.4% were previously treated with PCI, 3.3% were previously treated with CABG, 27.5% were current smokers, 43% had hypertension, 18.5% had diabetes, 5.4% had CHF, and 7.3% had suffered a previous stroke. When the cohort was divided into four 5 year periods (1998–2002, 2003–07, 2008–12, and 2013–17), there was an increased proportion of men from the first to the last period (65.8, 66.1, 68.7, and 69.5%), with a significant difference over the 20 years, P < 0.001. There were no major differences in age over the study period. The prevalence of hypertension (34.7%, 41.3%, 46.6%, and 50.5%, P < 0.001) as well as diabetes (18.1%, 18.7%, 17.8%, and 19.3%, P < 0.001) increased from the earliest to the latest period, whereas previous MI, CHF, and stroke decreased (20.0 vs. 12.4%, 6.8 vs. 3.7%, and 8.1 vs. 6.0%, in the first compared with the last time period, P < 0.001 overall) ( Table 1 ).

### Clinical characteristics at admission

In the total study cohort, 2.5% suffered from OHCA, and 4.6% had CS (cardiogenic shock). The rate of OHCA increased during the study period (1.1, 1.8, 3.1, and 4.1%, P < 0.001), whereas CS decreased (10.5, 3.6 2.0, and 2.0%, P < 0.001) ( Table 1 ). When the population was stratified according to time delay, STEMI patients presenting within 1 h had the highest rate of OHCA before admission (5.7% vs. 0.6%, P < 0.001) and the highest rate of CS on admission (6.6 and 4.0%, P < 0.001) (see Supplementary material online , Table S1 ).

### Reperfusion strategy and use of ambulance

The use of prehospital fibrinolysis peaked 2002 at 13% ( Figure 3 ). During the 20 year, the proportion of patients not treated with reperfusion therapy steadily decreased (27.0, 26.4, 12.7, and 9.1%, P < 0.001), whereas the proportion treated with PPCI increased (8.5, 47.5, 76.9, and 83.7%, P < 0.001) ( Table 2 ). Simultaneously, a higher proportion of patients was transported by ambulance directly to the CCU or cath lab (29.3, 49.9, 57.9, and 56.5%, P < 0.001). A higher proportion was transported by ambulance over time (71.2, 74.8, 82.2, and 79.2%, P < 0.001) ( Table 1 ).

### Characteristics at discharge

In the total cohort, 75.8% underwent left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ejection fraction (EF) assessment during hospital care, of which 46.5% had normal (>50%) LVEF. LVEF assessment increased during the 20 years (62.8, 70.3, 83.1, and 88.3%, P < 0.001). The proportion of patients with LVEF defined as normal increased over time (35.7, 44.1, 48.5, and 50.1%, P < 0.001) ( Table 2 ). A normal LVEF was present in most of patients presenting within 1 h (50.2%), whereas in the subgroup that delayed >12 h, 37.8% had normal LVEF when stratified by PHDT (see Supplementary material online , Table S2 ).

The use of evidence-based cardiovascular therapies such as P2Y12-receptor inhibitors (16.4, 73.2, 92.8, and 95.8%, P < 0.001), ACE inhibitors or ARBs (46.0, 64.8, 83.3, and 87.1%, P < 0.001), and statins (51.3, 81.1, 93.0, and 95.4%, P < 0.01) increased over time ( Table 2 ).

### Prehospital delay times

In the total study population, the median PHDT from onset of symptom to hospital admission was 150 min (IQR 80; 302). In the first 5 year period the median PHDT was 8 min longer than the last studied 5 year period, 148 (IQR 80; 305) vs. 140 min (IQR 85; 274). The last 5 year period had the shortest median PHDT whereas the longest delay was observed in 2003–2007 (156 min, IQR 83; 330). Between the first and the last 5 year periods, the median PHDT differed between men and women with 25 min, median PHDT for men 140 (IQR 80; 285) and women 165 min (IQR 90; 340), between patients <70 and ≥70 years with 30 min, 135 (IQR 77; 270) vs. 165 min (IQR 92; 340), and between patients with or without diabetes with 25 min, 170 (IQR 90; 353) vs. 145 min (IQR 84; 295) ( Table 3 ).

In a supplementary linear regression analysis, age (per year of increase), female sex, and the presence of diabetes were all independently associated with prolonged PHDT (see Supplementary material online , Table S4 ).

When stratifying the population according to PHDT (< 1 h, >1–2 h, >2–3 h, >3–6 h, >6–12 h, and >12 h), a gradual decrease in the proportion of men with increasing delay was shown (72.9, 69.4, 66.9, 65.2, 62.7% and 63.9% P < 0.001). Mean age increased (65.8, 67.7, 68.7, 69.6, 69.9 and 69.8 years, P < 0.001) and the prevalence of diabetes on admission (16.0, 16.6, 18.3, 20.3, 21.0 and 21.8%, P < 0.001) (see Supplementary material online , Table S1 ).

During the 20-year period, the PHDT curve was hump-shaped without any significant trend, and the differences in delay stratified by age, sex, and the presence of diabetes mellitus remained consistent. There was a significant increase in PHDT during the fibrinolytic era from 145 to 160 min whereas a decrease during the PPCI era from 151 to 140 min was observed ( Figure 2A–D ).

The Kruskal–Wallis test was significant ( P < 0.05) for all comparisons except for patients >80 years, P = 0.052. ### Association between prehospital delay time and survival

#### Short-term survival

With the cohort divided into six PHDT groups (<1 h, >1–2 h, >2–3 h, >3–6 h, >6–12 h, and >12 h), patients presenting within 1 h had the highest mortality the first 5 days and the fourth highest mortality at 14 days (8.1%, Figure 4A ). For the other groups 14 day mortality steadily increased with longer delay (6.5, 6.3, 7.2, 8.5, and 9.2%, P < 0.01). When the analysis was repeated after excluding patients with CS and OHCA, patients presenting within 1, 2, and 3 h had similar mortality rates (5.0%, 4.8%, 5.0%, 6.0%, 6.8% and 7.7%, P < 0.01%) (see Supplementary material online , Figure S1A ). After multivariable adjustment, PHDT was associated with 2% increased risk of 14 day mortality per hour (HR 1.018, 95% CI 1.011–1.025). In-hospital survivors, the multivariable adjusted risk was 4% per hour of PHDT increase (HR 1.042, 95% CI 1.025–1.059) ( Table 4 ). When stratifying the multivariable adjusted model by patients undergoing PPCI vs. not, PHDT was associated with 2% increased risk of 14 day mortality per hour in the PPCI group (HR 1.024, 95% CI 1.010–1.037) vs. 1% when PPCI was not performed (HR 1.009, 95% CI 1.002–1.017).

#### Long-term survival

Except for the group presenting within 1 h (absolute mortality rate 12.6%) after symptom onset, 1 year mortality steadily increased with longer delay (11.3, 11.9, 13.4, 15.4, and 16.1%, respectively, P < 0.01) which aligned with the estimated incidence of mortality ( Figure 4B ). In 5 year follow-up ( Figure 4C ), the lowest mortality rate was found for the group presenting within >1–2 h (18.1%), whereas it was higher in the group presenting within 1 h (19.0%). Mortality then increased with longer delay (19.7, 22.4, 25.0, and 24.9%, P < 0.01). When the analysis was repeated after excluding patients with CS and OHCA, patients presenting within 1 h demonstrated the lowest mortality at one-year (9.4, 9.6, 10.6, 12, 13.5, 14.6%) and at 5 year follow-up (16.4, 16.7, 18.4, 21.8, 23.2, 23.4%) (see Supplementary material online , Figure S1B and S1C ). When adjusting for confounders, the risk of 1 and 5 year mortality increased by approximately 1% per hour of prolonged PHDT (HR 1.011, 95% CI 1.006–1.016 and HR 1.009, 95% CI 1.005–1.013, respectively). Similar figures were seen in-hospital survivors (HR 1.016, 95% CI 1.009–1.023 and HR 1.009, 95% CI 1.004–1.014, respectively) ( Table 4 ). When stratifying the multivariable adjusted model by patients undergoing PPCI or not, the risk of 1 and 5 year mortality increased by approximately 2% per hour of prolonged PHDT in the PPCI group (HR 1.024 95% CI 1.014–1.034 and HR 1.020, 95% CI 1.012–1.028, respectively) vs. nearly 1% when PPCI was not performed (HR 1.009 95% CI 1.003–1.015 and HR 1.006 95% CI 1.001–1.011, respectively) (see Supplementary material online , Table S3 ).

## Discussion

In this nationwide cohort study of STEMI all-comers during a 20 year period, the key insights were as follows: (i) mortality incrementally increased with longer PHDT with the best prognosis among early presenters at long-term follow-up, (ii) the PHDT curve was hump-shaped without any significant trend of increase or decrease, and (iii) consistent differences in PHDT were found within sex, age, and diabetes subgroups.

Stratification of the cohort by PHDT showed that patients presenting within 1 h had the highest mortality up to 5 days after admission, likely due to being more critically ill indicated by higher rates of OHCA and CS at arrival. In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients suffering from these conditions, this pattern disappeared, and mortality increased per hour of prolonged delay. These results align with previous research. 3 , 25–27 A Swedish study found that very early presenters had more severe symptoms and more often left anterior descending artery occlusion, indicating a larger myocardial area at risk. 28 Rossello and colleagues, within the ESC framework, stress that acute coronary syndrome units should operate in integrated networks to ensure timely care. 7 In Sweden, the SWEDEHEART registry supports and enhance quality assessment of timely care and outcomes in STEMI patients. Nevertheless, geographic disparities remain, and hospital resources may vary in their capacity to manage high-risk patients, particularly those with CS. The high mortality among early presenters underscores the need to optimize strategies and ensure vigilant management of patients at risk for extensive myocardial damage.

Although patients with the longest delay had a low mortality rate within the first couple of days, they had the highest mortality by the end of 14 day follow-up. This trend continued at one and 5 years. Similarly, in a selective PPCI population, Jäger et al. found that PHDT independently impacted all-cause mortality by every 10 min over 3 years follow-up 18 Stratification by PPCI vs. not in this study further strengthened the findings of the importance of PHDT, which is predominantly shown in the PPCI treated group. This is not surprising as it is time to reperfusion that is primarily associated with mortality. In the current study, at 5 year follow-up, the lowest cumulative mortality rate of 18.1% was observed in patients with >1–2 h of PHDT. The mortality incrementally increased with longer delays, reaching a maximum of 25.0% in patients with more than 6 h of PHDT. When the analysis was repeated excluding patients with OHCA and CS, the 1 and 5 year mortality was similar for the two earliest presentation groups. Beyond this, there was a progressive rise with longer delay. After extensive multivariable adjustments, every hour of longer PHDT was associated with 2% higher mortality at 14 days and 1% at 1 year and 5 years. These findings emphasize the critical importance of reducing PHDT to improve outcomes, minimizing the risk of heart failure and lethal arrhythmias.

Over the last decades, there have been significant changes in STEMI management aimed at reducing total ischaemic time. The focus has primarily been on reducing door-to-balloon time (DTB) and most PPCI patients can now be treated promptly. 29 Mortality has decreased, in parallel with shortening DTB times and increased use of PPCI. 30 , 31 Nevertheless, the largest part of the total ischaemic part is PHDT, which has been more hard to impact. 32 In the current study no trend of PHDT decrease or increase was shown during the 20 years as the curve was hump-shaped. During the fibrinolytic era, PHDT increased by 15 min, but since PPCI implementation, PHDT significantly decreased. 24 This change of strategy affected EMS routines in Sweden, with STEMI diagnosis made in the prehospital setting, redirecting ambulances to cath lab and bypassing ER. This may explain why PHDT initially increased when new routines were being established but subsequently decreased. Additionally, the use of ambulance increased during the PPCI period, contributing to the decrease in PHDT.

Still, PHDT averaging 2 h and 30 min is far too long. These findings emphasize that efforts are still needed to shorten patient delay, the main component of PHDT, persistently difficult to impact. 8 , 23 Notably, over the entire 20 year period, approximately half an hour min longer PHDT was consistently observed in patients aged ≥70 years, women, and patients with diabetes. Previous studies have identified these patients as having prolonged delay in STEMI. 9 , 24 These factors were also shown to be independently associated with prolonged PHDT in our study. Possible explanations for the prolonged delay in elderly could be that they often live alone, have lower symptom recognition, and use self-medication more frequently. 9 , 25 Longer delays for patients with diabetes may be due to less alarming symptoms. 10 , 11 , 26 Women also delayed longer than men, with a previous study showing that women had more than five times higher risk of present with STEMI without chest pain. 33 Another study revealed that women waited in median 90 min before FMC compared with 66 min in men and that men were more often recommended to call 112 by a bystanders. 9 In addition, prior research has shown that the choice of FMC substantially influences PHDT overall and differs in groups vulnerable for prolonged delay such as women, elderly, and patients with diabetes. 6 The lack of improvement over two decades highlights the complexity these groups face accessing timely care.

### Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study is the complete and thoroughly monitored registration of hospitalized STEMI patients in Sweden. All hospitals participating in SWEDEHEART and the registry offering a high coverage of data as well regularly independent monitors. This cohort represents all-comers data and describes the clinical reality and therefor provides high internal validity, closely resembling an all-comers population, including a substantial proportion of women, elderly individuals, and patients with significant comorbidity. 34 Furthermore, the present study covers long-term follow-up of the vital status of STEMI patients, with minor loss to follow-up. Several limitations merit attention. Firstly, although the representativeness of Swedish STEMI patients is excellent, the results should not be automatically extrapolated to other countries. This is particularly relevant for PHDT which are known to vary considerably across regions due to differences in insurance systems, and healthcare organization. Although we argue that the impact of delay on mortality is likely similar globally, this assumption cannot be confirmed with certainty. Secondly, in observational studies there is an inherent risk of residual unmeasured or unknown confounders leading to cautiousness regarding causality. We neither had information on occluded coronary arteries nor variables on MI location. Instead, we have used OHCA as well as CS as proxy variables for conditions with larger myocardial area at risk, and we have performed a sensitivity analyses excluding patients suffering from these conditions. Furthermore, we had no information on FMC, as the register only contain information on transportation mode and site of admission. Thirdly, the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated for the main exposure variable using visual inspection of Kaplan–Meier curves. Formal testing for all covariates was not performed, as many were categorical and less likely to exhibit meaningful time-dependent effects. However, we acknowledge that this approach does not fully rule out violations of the assumption. Finally, as with all registry studies, coding errors may exist causing misclassification bias.

## Conclusion

To summarize, our study on STEMI all-comers reveals that each hour of PHDT increases short-term mortality by 2% and long-term mortality by 1%. We conclude that PHDT is an independent risk factor for short- and long-term mortality. Despite significant changes in STEMI management, there has been no substantial decrease in PHDT over the last two decades, although it is reassuring that PHDT decreased during the PPCI era. However, PHDT remain far too long, and continuous efforts are needed to diminish patient delays to minimize myocardial damage, along with its complications such as premature death and heart failure. Future interventions should focus on patient actions, especially targeting risk groups such as women, the elderly, and patients with diabetes.
