# Influence of C YP2D6 Genotypes and Phenotypes on the Plasma Levels and Clinical Response to Aripiprazole

- PMCID: PMC12996889
- DOI: 10.1093/schbul/sbaf076
- Primary source: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12996889
- Retrieved: 2026-08-07T04:13:33.555647Z

## Abstract

Abstract Background The antipsychotic aripiprazole is mainly metabolized by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6. The main objective of this study was to evaluate the influence of CYP2D6 phenotypes on aripiprazole plasma levels and treatment duration. Design 466 patients treated with aripiprazole for up to 12 months and with at least one aripiprazole plasma level in steady-state condition were selected. CYP2D6 genotypes and phenoconversion to poor metabolizer status due to strong CYP2D6 inhibition were considered. Aripiprazole plasma level-to-dose ratios and treatment duration up to discontinuation, defined as switching to another psychotropic drug and/or stopping the follow-up, were analyzed using robust linear models and Cox regression, respectively. Akaike variable selection was applied. Results CYP2D6 poor metabolizers (genetically determined n = 19 and phenoconverted, n = 52) showed higher aripiprazole concentration-to-dose ( P < .001) and aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole concentration-to-dose ( P < .001) ratios when compared to normal metabolizers ( n = 255). CYP2D6 extreme metabolizers (ie, poor and ultrarapid metabolizers) had higher risk of treatment discontinuation versus intermediate and normal metabolizers after 3, 6, and 12 months of treatment (HR: 2.08, 1.75, 1.59, respectively; P = .013, P = .019, and P = .047, respectively). For a pharmacogenetic-guided treatment, the number of patients needed to genotype to prevent 1 patient from aripiprazole discontinuation was 15. Conclusion CYP2D6 poor metabolism was associated with increased aripiprazole and aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole concentrations-to-dose. CYP2D6 extreme phenotypes were associated with increased risk of treatment discontinuation over 1 year of treatment. This finding supports the applicability of pre-emptive CYP2D6 genotyping, which is expected to decrease aripiprazole adverse events and inefficacy that would probably lead to treatment discontinuation. Keywords: aripiprazole, genetic polymorphisms, CYP2D6 , phenotype, pre-emptive genotyping

## Introduction

Aripiprazole is a widely prescribed atypical antipsychotic, available in both oral tablets and long-acting formulations. 1 Aripiprazole is mainly metabolized by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6, and partially by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 into the active metabolite dehydroaripiprazole. 1 Depending mainly on CYP2D6 and partially on CYP3A4/5 genetic polymorphisms, aripiprazole pharmacokinetics has high intervariability, with individuals metabolizing aripiprazole more rapidly or poorly than others. Moreover, since aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole are also substrates of the transport P-glycoprotein at the blood–brain barrier, polymorphisms of the corresponding ABCB1 gene can also affect central or peripheral aripiprazole concentrations. 2 Thus, aripiprazole pharmacokinetics and, therefore, its influence on therapeutic response and/or adverse effects, may depend on several genetic polymorphisms (ie, CYP2D6 , CYP3A4/5 , and ABCB1 genes) and on additional factors such as age, cotreatments, and comorbidities. 1

The latter clinical factors (eg, age, cotreatments) along with pharmacogenetic variability constitute a great challenge also for the selection of the most adapted antipsychotic treatment and posology. Consequently, international guidelines endorsing the relevance of CYP genotyping to adapt psychotropic treatment have been published. 3 Since aripiprazole is a widely prescribed antipsychotic, these guidelines emphasize the potential utility of pre-emptive CYP2D6 genotyping to adapt aripiprazole treatment.

Whether aripiprazole dosage should be adapted according to CYP2D6 phenotypes has previously been debated. Indeed, a retrospective study including 166 patients receiving the long-acting formulation and 469 patients receiving the oral formulation reported higher exposure to aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole (active moiety) among CYP2D6 poor (PM) and intermediate metabolizers (IM) when compared with ultrarapid (UM) and normal metabolizers (NM), 4 which led to a recommendation by the Food and Drug Administration to halve the usual dose among PM, 5 and to a recommendation by the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group to administer to PM no more than 10 mg/day (oral form) or 300 mg/month (depot formulation). 6

On the other hand, the impact of CYP2D6 phenotypes on clinical response to aripiprazole and its adverse effects is still unclear. A study including 277 pediatric patients showed increased treatment discontinuation due to side effects in PM when compared to other CYP2D6 groups. 7 Moreover, although a retrospective study including 890 patients showed a 1.6-fold increase in aripiprazole active moiety exposure in CYP2D6 PM and IM versus NM and UM, no association was found between aripiprazole discontinuation (defined as switching to another antipsychotic within 1 year from the last available aripiprazole therapeutic drug monitoring) and CYP2D6 phenotypes defined by genetics only. 8

With a large Swiss cohort of aripiprazole-treated patients, the main aim of the present study is to examine the influence of CYP2D6 phenotypes on aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole plasma levels and treatment duration up to discontinuation. Secondarily, the influence of CYP3A4/A5 and ABCB1 most common genetic polymorphisms on these outcomes are evaluated.

## Methods

### Patients

Patients were selected from the PsyMetab cohort, a longitudinal noninterventional study approved by the ethics committee of the Canton of Vaud (protocol ID: 2017-01301) and of the Canton of Geneva (protocol ID: 14-259). After signed informed consent is received, PsyMetab collects clinical and genetic data of psychiatric patients followed-up at the public hospital of Lausanne and Geneva, and at a private ambulatory clinic Les Toises. Clinical data (eg, smoking status, weight, adverse events) is collected at 0, 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after psychotropic treatment starts, 9 and drug plasma levels are measured after 1, 3, and 12 months of treatment (additional plasma levels are measured during the follow-up according to clinical needs). From this cohort, 466 patients treated with oral aripiprazole for at least 2 weeks and/or with long-acting formulations for at least 4 months to allow steady-state conditions were included in the present study. The long-acting dose was converted into an oral daily dose as follows: daily oral dose = (long-acting dose/0.85) * (1/dose interval in days). Since patients could have multiple plasma levels of aripiprazole, only the most recent result with complete clinical information (eg, dose, duration between oral intake or depot injection and blood sampling) was retained. Treatment discontinuation was also considered and defined as stopping aripiprazole follow-up (due to switching or to unknown causes) or switching from aripiprazole to another psychotropic drug.

### Plasma Levels

Aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole plasma levels were obtained by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled to a single quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS) with electrospray ionization (LC-MS Agilent 1100, until August 12, 2019 10 ) or by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC, Waters ACQUITY UHPLC system) coupled to a tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Xevo TQ-XS with electrospray ionization, from August 13, 2019; the full method description is available on request). Both methods used a stable isotope-labeled internal standard for each analyte and were validated according to international guidelines in a laboratory with an ISO 15189 accreditation. Limit of quantification for both aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole was 5 ng/ml by HPLC–MS and 1 ng/ml by UHPLC–MS/MS. After blood drawing in EDTA-tubes and centrifugation, plasma samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis. For 441 patients, aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole concentrations were analyzed during routine therapeutic drug monitoring shortly after blood drawing. For 25 patients, stored blood samples were analyzed for the purpose of the present study in April 2022. Since the latter samples were analyzed after storage of up to 8 years ( n = 25), the stability of aripiprazole and its metabolite were confirmed by reanalyzing samples previously quantified during routine therapeutic drug monitoring (data not shown).

### Genotyping and Determinations of Phenotypes

DNA extraction was performed using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit on QIAcube (Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). Most genotypes were obtained from the Global Screening Array (GSA) version 2 (patients recruited up to January 22, 2019, n = 368) and 3 (patients recruited after January 22, 2019, n = 98) with Multiple Disease Option chip. For CYP2D6, the most frequent alleles in the Caucasian population 11 associated with complete loss ( CYP2D6*3, rs35742686; CYP2D6*4, rs3892097), or with reduced CYP2D6 function ( CYP2D6*10, rs1065852; CYP2D6*41, rs28371725) were obtained. 8 , 12 CYP3A4*22 (rs35599367) , associated with reduced CYP3A4 activity, CYP3A5*3 (rs776746) associated with a loss of CYP3A5 activity, and ABCB1 3435C>T (rs1045642) and 2677C>T/A (rs2032582) polymorphisms, with the T alleles associated with lower P-glycoprotein activity, were also obtained. 13–15 Lastly, CYP2D6*5 (total gene deletion) and CYP2D6*6 (rs5030655) both associated with a loss of CYP2D6 activity, and CYP2D6*XN (duplications) associated with increased CYP2D6 activity were analyzed using TaqMan Assays as previously described 15 (ABI PRISM 7000; Applied Biosystems, Rotkreuz, Switzerland, up to 2014 and ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System; Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific from 2014).

From these alleles, CYP2D6 phenotypes were determined using Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium and Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group consensus guidelines ( Table S1 ). 16 Patients taking strong CYP2D6 inhibitors (ie, bupropion [ n = 4], fluoxetine [ n = 23], paroxetine [ n = 7], and levomepromazine [ n = 2]) at the retained plasma level analysis, were phenoconverted into poor metabolizers (pPM) regardless of their initial phenotype. Since levomepromazine is frequently prescribed on a pro re nata basis, this interaction was only considered when levomepromazine was detected in the plasma samples (2 patients out of 5). Of note, whether strong CYP2D6 inhibitor intake started at the beginning or during aripiprazole treatment was unknown. Due to the lack of evidence about the magnitude of CYP2D6 inhibition by weak inhibitors, phenoconversion in the present study was limited to strong inhibitors.

### Statistical Analysis

Aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole plasma level-related variables, aripiprazole discontinuation rates defined as switching from aripiprazole and/or stopping the follow-up, and reported adverse effects were compared between CYP2D6 phenotypes using Kruskal–Wallis or Wilcoxon tests for continuous outcomes, and Chi-2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical outcomes as appropriate. Survival analysis followed by univariate logrank tests were performed to assess the treatment duration to discontinuation. Robust linear regression models were performed on aripiprazole and dehydroaripiprazole concentrations and on concentration/dose ratios, which were BoxCox transformed to reduce their deviation from a normal distribution. For treatment duration up to discontinuation at 3, 6, and 12 months, Cox regression was employed, and logistic regression for the incidence of adverse effects. For linear, logistic and Cox regression, the R function stepAIC, which selects the model minimizing the Akaike criteria (AIC), was used to select a model that only kept the most relevant variables among: age, sex, ethnic group (defined using genetic data as previously described 17 ), setting of care (in- and out-patients), main diagnosis, patients’ weight, aripiprazole daily dose, galenic formulation (oral or long-acting), other antipsychotic(s) or antidepressant(s) as comedication(s), CYP2D6 weak or strong inhibitors, CYP3A4 inhibitors and the polymorphisms of interest (ie, CYP2D6 phenotypes, CYP3A4 *22, CYP3A5 *3 and ABCB1 3435 and 2677 genotypes). Of note, the time interval between the last intake of aripiprazole and blood sampling was not considered in linear models, due aripiprazole’s long half-life. Moreover, the average number of patients to genotype to find one CYP2D6 extreme phenotype (PM or UM) was calculated considering PM and UM proportion in our population (ie, 8.5%) and the sensitivity of the genetic test as follows 18 : 1/(sensitivity of genetic test* proportion of PM and UM in the population). Although the test sensitivity for detecting PM among Caucasians is between 95% and 99%, 19 the overall test sensitivity for detecting PM and UM was conservatively lowered in the present calculation to 80%, to take into account possible additional and/or rare and/or nondetected mutations which could decrease 20 or increase 21 CYP2D6 activity. A P value of less than .05 was considered significant. STATA/IC version 16.1 software (Stata Corp, TX, USA) was used for data management. Statistical analysis was performed on R software version 4.2.1.

## Results

Clinical and genetic parameters are reported in Table 1 . Of the 466 included patients, 4.1% were CYP2D6 PM, 30.5% IM, 60.9% NM, and 4.5% UM. All polymorphisms were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium ( Table S2 ). After phenoconversion, 52 patients were considered poor metabolizers, versus 19 PM defined by genetics only. Although mean daily doses were significantly different between CYP2D6 phenotypes when considering phenoconversion ( Table S3 ), multiple comparison did not reveal a dose difference between NM versus the other phenotypes. Considering genetics only, IM had 56% increased mean aripiprazole levels, 44% increased mean aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole levels, and 27% higher aripiprazole-to-dose ratios, respectively, when compared with NM ( Table S3 and Figure 1 ). Moreover, dehydroaripiprazole to aripiprazole ratios were 47% decreased in PM when compared with NM. Considering phenoconversion, pPM and IMs had levels of aripiprazole increased by 56% and 61%, and dehydroaripiprazole-to-aripiprazole ratio decreased by 46% and 27%, respectively, when compared with NM ( Figure 1 ).

Aripiprazole-to-dose and aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole-to-dose ratios ( Table 2 ) were increased in CYP2D6 IM ( P < .001 and P = .010, respectively) versus NM, and in patients with aripiprazole long-acting formulation versus patients taking the oral form only ( P = .014 and P = .004, respectively). The hazard of aripiprazole discontinuation at 3 months of treatment was significantly higher in PM and UM (35% rate) than in NM and IM (22% rate; P = .045 at univariate logrank test, see Figure 2 ). After phenoconversion, considering pPM and UM together versus NM and IM, no difference was observed (29% vs 22% switching rate, respectively, P = .26; Figure S1 ). Cox regression ( Table 3 , see Table S5 for full models) showed that PM and UM phenotypes had higher risk of treatment discontinuation at 3, 6, and 12 months of treatment (HR: 2.08, 1.75, 1.59, respectively; P = .013, P = .019, and P = .047, respectively). On the other hand, when considering phenoconversion, pPM and UM had higher risk of treatment discontinuation only at 6 months (HR: 1.51; P = .040), although a trend was observed at 3 months (HR: 1.62; P = .073). Of note, no violation of Cox proportional hazard was found for CYP2D6 extreme phenotypes (with or without phenoconversion, data not shown). CYP3A5*3, CYP3A4*22, and ABCB1 genotypes were neither associated nor retained by AIC model selection when considering treatment discontinuation at 3, 6, or 12 months. A first sensitivity analysis considering only patients switching from aripiprazole (ie, considering patients stopping the follow-up for unknown reasons as censored) at 3, 6, and 12 months of treatment was performed, reporting similar results only for nonphenoconverted PM and UM (see Figure S2 and Table S6 ). Moreover, a second sensitivity analysis also reported similar results for nonphenoconverted PM and UM when excluding patients taking the long-acting formulation (for 3, 6, and 12 months: HR 1.95, 1.67, and 1.52; P = .027, .037, and .076, respectively) or strong CYP2D6 inhibitors (HR 2.34, 1.73, 1.51; P = .008, P = .036, P = .099, respectively).

Given the low number of patients reporting adverse effects (see Table S3 for frequencies), the influence of variables of interest was evaluated only for the most reported adverse effects, that is, sedation ( N = 44 reporting this adverse effect). CYP2D6 phenotypes, CYP3A4/5 and ABCB1 genotypes were not associated with this outcome nor retained by AIC model selection (data not shown). Nevertheless, weak CYP2D6 inhibitor intake was associated with 2.0 increased odds ( P = .046) of experiencing sedation when considering nonphenoconverted patients in the AIC model selection.

Lastly, the average number of patients needed to genotype (NNG) to find one CYP2D6 extreme phenotype (PM or UM) was 15, that is, 15 patients supposedly needed to prevent one case of aripiprazole discontinuation.

## Discussion

With a longitudinal design, the present study reported a greater risk of aripiprazole discontinuation for CYP2D6 extreme metabolizers within the first year of treatment. Specifically, PM and UM presented an increased risk of treatment discontinuation when considering 3, 6, or 12 months of treatment. On the other hand, for UM and pPM (ie, when considering the administration of strong CYP2D6 inhibitors), an increased risk was shown only for 6 months, the nonsignificant result at 3 and 12 months being possibly explained by the prescription of CYP2D6 inhibitors only during part of aripiprazole treatment. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess aripiprazole discontinuation within 3 and 6 months of treatment. Discontinuation within 12 months from the last available aripiprazole plasma level measurement was previously evaluated in a retrospective study, 8 with no significant differences between CYP2D6 genotypes. Since treatment duration was not taken into account in the latter study, 8 the lack of influence of CYP2D6 genotypes on aripiprazole discontinuation could be explained by the inclusion of patients with marked differences in treatment durations (eg, a few weeks to much longer periods even exceeding 1 year), with patients reporting poor tolerance (possibly associated with PM status) and/or lack of efficacy (possibly associated with UM status) more likely to drop out at earlier stages. This hypothesis is supported by the present study. Indeed, as previously mentioned, although Cox proportional hazard was respected at 3, 6, or 12 months of treatment (ie, the risk was time-independent), a higher hazard ratio was reported at 3 months versus 12 months (2.08 vs 1.59, respectively). Of note, this result was also confirmed by a sensitivity analysis considering only patients switching from aripiprazole (ie, censoring patients stopping the follow-up for unknown reasons), the hazard ratios being 2.49 and 1.92 at 3 versus 12 months, respectively. Although the reasons for treatment discontinuation could not be retrieved in the present study, a study conducted in 277 pediatric patients showed a significantly higher percentage of discontinuation due to side effects in PM compared with NM (no information was provided on UM status nor on treatment duration). 7 It must be mentioned that, in the present study, side effects could not be effectively evaluated due to the high proportion of incomplete data, preventing the evaluation of an association between aripiprazole discontinuation and adverse effects.

The present results also reported increased aripiprazole and aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole concentrations (either alone or considering their ratio with aripiprazole dose) among pPM versus NM, which in agreement with a previous study reporting 1.6-fold increased active moiety in PM and IM. 8 These results are also in line with several previous studies, reporting increased aripiprazole and aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole plasma levels and decreased metabolic ratios (dehydroaripiprazole/aripiprazole) among CYP2D6 PM and IM. 4 , 22–24 Therefore, the present results support the current recommendations to adapt aripiprazole dose for CYP2D6 PM and for patients receiving strong CYP2D6 inhibitors. 5 , 6 Of note, in the present study plasma levels (either alone or considering their ratio with aripiprazole dose) were not influenced by UM status, probably due to an insufficient sample size ( N = 21 UM). Hence, whether aripiprazole dose should be adapted also for UM remains to be investigated. CYP3A5*3 and CYP3A4*22 alleles did not influence plasma levels of aripiprazole nor aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole in the present study, in agreement with previous studies including Asian patients, which did not find any associations between aripiprazole plasma level and CYP3A4/5 genotypes. 22 , 25 , 26 On the other hand, CYP3A5 *3/*3 genotype was associated with reduced aripiprazole-to-dehydroaripiprazole ratio in a cohort of 148 Spanish healthy volunteers receiving a single dose of 10 mg aripiprazole, the discrepancy in the results possibly being explained by the different cohort sizes, aripiprazole doses, or subject profiles (ie, single doses in healthy volunteers vs psychiatric patients taking aripiprazole for several weeks or months). 2 Lastly, our findings showed no association between ABCB1 3435 C>T or ABCB1 2677 C>T polymorphisms and aripiprazole or aripiprazole plus dehydroaripiprazole plasma levels, which is in agreement with previous studies. 2 , 26

Adverse effects were not systematically recorded and, because of their overall low prevalence, the association with the genotypes of interest was tested only for the most frequently reported adverse effect (ie, sedation). Sedation was not associated with CYP2D6, CYP3A4/5 , nor ABCB1 genotypes. Interestingly, we found higher odds of experiencing sedation for patients taking weak, but not strong CYP2D6 inhibitors, this result probably explained by the lower number of patients taking the strong inhibitors ( n = 36 vs n = 155).

Since the number of patients needed to genotype to identify 1 PM or UM patient taking aripiprazole was estimated at 15, we could set a base-case scenario to determine whether CYP2D6 genetic analysis might be recommended on a larger scale. Considering a dose–response meta-analysis of antipsychotic drugs for acute schizophrenia, CYP2D6 PM and UM patients should benefit from the ~50% reductions in aripiprazole-related adverse events and lack of efficacy following dose adjustments. 27 , 28 From a broader health economic perspective, antipsychotic dose optimizations among PM and UM could be associated with shorter hospital length of stay (LOS) due to better tolerability and efficacy. Hence, the costs of in-depth CYP2D6 genetic analysis in Switzerland (€ 400, assuming 1€ = 1CHF [state Aug 2024]) and extra days (€ 705) within the LOS were considered. Using a cost-minimization approach, the additional 22.5 days between the median LOS for CYP2D6 extremes (61 days, N = 16 with available LOS data) versus nonextreme CYP2D6 metabolizers (median LOS: 38.5 days, N = 132 with available LOS data; of note the difference between the 2 LOS was not statistically different probably due to the low sample size), were associated with an extra cost of € 15 862.5 (22.5 days * cost charged for 1 additional day). Considering the costs of CYP2D6 genotyping to prevent 50% of the adverse events or inefficacy (ie, €400.5 *NNG*2), the trade-off was favorable for the preventive genetic test to avoid aripiprazole discontinuation and limit prolonged LOS associated with adverse effects and/or lack of efficacy. Furthermore, CYP2D6 genetic testing is performed once in a lifetime and can be used to individualize pharmacological treatments for other CYP2D6-metabolized drugs in patients who have potentially undergone multiple treatments or hospitalizations. Nevertheless, this estimation needs to be confirmed by future prospective cost-effectiveness studies considering supplementary costs such as societal costs for nonworking hospitalized patients. Of note, the potential clinical utility of combining therapeutic drug monitoring and CYP2D6 genetic testing for the prescription of antipsychotics has been recently discussed 3 and is also suggested in the present study, as phenoconversion impacted aripiprazole blood levels among NM, IM, and UM. The clinical and economic advantages and disadvantages of combining therapeutic drug monitoring with CYP2D6 genetic testing should therefore be evaluated in further studies.

This study has several limitations. First, because of its noninterventional retrospective nature, all possible causes for aripiprazole discontinuation (eg, adverse effects and/or lack of efficacy) could not be considered. Moreover, as in most observational studies, only a low incidence of adverse effects was reported, probably due to missing data. Data on protective (eg, family involvement) or risk factors (eg, poor illness insight) for treatment discontinuations, 29 which could have affected our results, were also missing, as well as scores and indicators of treatment response (eg, PANSS score). Rare CYP2D6 and CYP3A4/5 alleles, susceptible to altering enzyme activity, were not considered. However, because of their low frequencies, 20 , 30 it is unlikely that their inclusion would have significantly altered the present results. Lastly, whether strong CYP2D6 inhibitor intake started at the beginning or during aripiprazole treatment (and for how long) was unknown, potentially explaining the differing results on treatment discontinuation between genetics-only and phenoconverted phenotypes. On the other hand, the large sample size and the longitudinal design are the main strengths of the present study, which could highlight an association between CYP2D6 extreme metabolizers and aripiprazole discontinuation after 3, 6, and 12 months of treatment.

In conclusion, the present study confirms the important influence of CYP2D6 on the metabolism of aripiprazole. Importantly, it highlights that extreme CYP2D6 phenotypes (PM or UM) are associated with a higher risk of treatment discontinuation within the first year of treatment. This finding supports the applicability of pre-emptive CYP2D6 genotyping, which is expected to decrease aripiprazole adverse events and inefficacy that would probably lead to treatment discontinuation.
