## Q1. Simulation-study methodology and regulatory expectations: what must a simulation report contain, how many replicates, how is Monte Carlo error reported?

### Takeaway
Every authoritative text (Morris 2019 ADEMP; FDA 2019 adaptive-design guidance and 2020 CID guidance; ICH E20 draft 2025; FDA Bayesian draft Jan 2026; CDE 2026 No. 6 and its draft) asks for the same skeleton: a stated decision question, candidate designs including a benchmark, a justified scenario grid, an explicit data-generating process, replicate counts with a rationale, seeds and code, example trials, and limitations. The replicate-count convention for type I error is 100,000 per scenario (FDA 2019, E20, the CDE draft, Granholm 2025); 10,000 is accepted for power and other quantities; the generic methodology paper only requires that the count be chosen from a target Monte Carlo SE.

### Cited Findings

#### Morris, White, Crowther 2019 (ADEMP) [PR], partly read
- Statistics in Medicine 2019;38(11):2074-2102; ADEMP = Aims, Data-generating mechanisms, Estimands/targets, Methods, Performance measures. Targets may also be a null hypothesis, model selection, prediction, or design; methods must be "serious contenders". — [PMC6492164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6492164); preprint at [arXiv 1712.03198](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.03198)
- Reporting items it recommends: describe the study in ADEMP terms with rationale for each choice; report n_sim and how it was decided; report Monte Carlo SE; put competing methods side by side; publish code; give formulae for less-used performance measures. — [PMC6492164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6492164)
- Choose n_sim from a required Monte Carlo SE: n_sim = coverage x (1 - coverage) / MCSE_req^2. Worked values in the paper: 95% coverage with required MCSE 0.5% gives 1,900; worst case (50% coverage) with MCSE 0.5% gives 10,000; for MCSE of bias below 0.005 with Var(estimate) <= 0.04 gives 1,600, which yields MCSE(coverage) of about 0.54% at 95% coverage and at most 1.25% at 50% coverage. Formulae: MCSE(bias) = sqrt(Var/n_sim); MCSE of coverage or power = sqrt(p(1-p)/n_sim). — [PMC6492164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6492164)
- Its review of Statistics in Medicine volume 34 (264 articles; 199, 75%, had a simulation study; 100 randomly selected examined): 93 of 100 did not mention Monte Carlo SEs and 7 did; common n_sim were 500 and 1000; of 87 that reported n_sim only 4 justified it; performance measures reported: bias 63/80, coverage 42/79, empirical SE 31/78, type I error 31/95, power 28/95. — [PMC6492164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6492164)
- ADEMP-PreReg template (Siepe, Bartos, Morris, Boulesteix, Heck, Pawel), Psychological Methods 2024, doi 10.1037/met0000695: adapts ADEMP to planning/preregistration/reporting, adds MCSE formulae and repetition-number calculation; its review of 321 articles (2021-22) found ~31% reported a simulation study and many lacked justification of repetitions and MCSE. [PR; search-summary only, not opened] — [UCL/MCML record](https://mcml.ai/publications/sbm+24/); [PMC7616844](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7616844)
- Pawel, Kook, Reeve, "Pitfalls and potentials in simulation studies" (Biometrical Journal 2023): questionable research practices in comparative simulation studies can make any method look superior; argues for preregistered simulation protocols and shared code. [PR/PP; search-summary only] — [arXiv 2203.13076](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.13076)
- I found no 2023-2026 reporting checklist written specifically for trial-design simulation reports other than the regulator texts below and the Granholm guide; ADEMP-PreReg is methodological-research oriented (psychology), not trial design.

#### ACE: CONSORT extension for adaptive designs (Dimairo et al., BMJ 2020;369:m115) [PR], partly read (first ~100k of 422k characters)
- Defines simulation as "a computational procedure performed using a computer program to evaluate statistical properties of the design" and operating characteristics as relating to "behaviour of the trial design". Item 7a (modified) "How sample size and operating characteristics were determined", with explanation "describe the use of statistical simulations when relevant" and that design-parameter assumptions "should be clearly stated and supported with evidence if possible"; authors are encouraged to supply a simulation report where simulations estimated bias or operating characteristics (items 12b, 24b/24c). Item 3b (new): type of adaptive design, pre-planned adaptations, and the statistical information informing them. — [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/369/bmj.m115.full)

#### FDA, Adaptive Designs for Clinical Trials of Drugs and Biologics, final guidance, December 2019 [REG-final], read
- Section VI.A "Simulations in Adaptive Design Planning": simulations select number/timing of interim looks, set critical values, compare alternative designs, and "estimate trial operating characteristics and ... demonstrate that these operating characteristics meet desired levels". Analytic theory suffices for fixed designs and traditional group sequential designs; simulation is for designs where distributions are intractable (several adaptations, predictive-probability timing). — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- Type I error by simulation: simulate under "a series of assumptions compatible with the null"; there are infinitely many null scenarios; use medical plus mathematical (monotonicity) arguments to pick a limited set. Worked example: 2-year mortality trial in a median-6-month-survival disease; clinicians rule out placebo mortality below 75%; simulate a grid of 75, 80, 85, 90, 95, 99%; if each estimate is below 0.025 type I error is "adequately controlled" over that range; if observed placebo mortality is 50% "additional simulations would be required". Nuisance parameters include variance, control response probability, and operational ones such as accrual speed. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- Replicates: "Using 100,000 iterations per scenario ... ensures a 95% confidence interval for estimated Type I error probability with a width of approximately +/-0.1%"; 10,000 may suffice with a fine grid where every scenario is below the target, or when the upper CI bound is below the target; use different seeds for different scenarios; power, expected sample size, duration and bias need fewer scenarios and iterations. (Own arithmetic check: at p = 0.025, 100,000 runs give a 95% half-width of 0.00097.) — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- Multiple endpoints: simulating all endpoints needs the correlation structure; assuming independence gives an upper bound for Bonferroni/Holm. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- Section VIII.B (documentation prior to the trial): rationale for design versus "alternative adaptive and non-adaptive designs"; adaptation plan; pre-specified methods; operating characteristics "typically ... Type I error probability; power; expected, minimum, and maximum sample size; bias of treatment effect estimates; and coverage of confidence intervals"; where simulation is primary or sole technique a "detailed simulation report" with: overall design description; example trials (positive at original size, stopped for futility at first look, positive after size increase); set of parameter configurations "including a justification of the adequacy of the choices"; number of iterations per scenario and rationale; results per scenario; code (readable, commented, with the random seeds); summary conclusions. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- Bayesian adaptive designs (VI.B): for Bayesian inference with informative priors "Bayesian statistical properties are more informative than Type I error probability"; simulations "particularly resource-intensive". — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)

#### FDA, Interacting with the FDA on Complex Innovative Trial Designs, December 2020 [REG-final], read
- "A common feature of many CIDs is the need for simulations rather than mathematical formulae"; required content for a CID proposal includes "a detailed evaluation of the operating characteristics ... including its chance of producing erroneous conclusions", prior-sensitivity for Bayesian inference, and "a simulation report if simulations are used"; plans for simulations are best discussed at End-of-Phase-2 meetings (scenarios and "underlying trial assumptions, such as accrual rate and likely control group outcomes"), and extra Type C meetings may be granted for extensive simulation. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/130897/download)

#### ICH E20 draft "Adaptive Designs for Clinical Trials" [REG-draft], read (FDA-hosted copy)
- Status: Step 1 and Step 2a/b sign-off June 2025, regional consultation completed Nov 2025, Step 3 and Step 4 sign-off planned Feb 2027 (EWG work plan dated 10 July 2026; the work plan lists a March 2021 "Simulation team" milestone). — [ICH work plan](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH53_E20_EWG_Workplan_2026_0812.pdf); draft text [FDA-hosted E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- Section 5.2 "Planning, Conducting, and Reporting Simulation Studies" (the most complete published checklist): define objectives "directly related to the decisions that will be made"; select designs that "include a benchmark design and analysis approach, i.e., a design with well-understood operating characteristics such as a non-adaptive or group sequential design"; operating characteristics "generally include the Type I error probability, expected sample size, expected trial duration, power, coverage of confidence intervals, and bias and mean squared error", and look at variability, min/max, not only the mean; scenarios must "cover the plausible range" of treatment effects, nuisance parameters (e.g. SD) and operational assumptions (enrolment, dropout) justified from "data from previous trials, publications, results from extrapolations, and expert input", "attempts should be made to quantify uncertainty and identify potential biases"; a grid needs justification that its range covers plausible values and that it is fine enough (e.g. monotonicity); where simulation is critical for type I error "additional justification is expected"; data-generating process may be "a normal distribution with mean and variance obtained from previous trials" or a model fit on earlier trial data; "100,000 or more repetitions per scenario" may be needed for type I error and fewer for power; documented seed. — [FDA-hosted E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- The 11-item report required before the trial: (1) key questions; (2) designs and analysis options evaluated; (3) operating characteristics chosen; (4) existing knowledge and supporting data for scenarios; (5) parameter configurations with clinical justification that they cover the plausible range; (6) implementation details, data-generating process, repetitions per scenario with justification; (7) software and code with example call and starting seed; (8) overall results and conclusions (interactive graphs encouraged); (9) examples of single simulated trials with different adaptations and conclusions; (10) aspects that limit interpretation (e.g. uncertainty in assumptions); (11) clinical discussion of whether results address the key questions; "explicit links between clinical and statistical assumptions and results". — [FDA-hosted E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- Section 5.3 (flagged "not fully harmonized"): Bayesian borrowing needs justification that alternatives without borrowing are infeasible, a list of all potentially relevant sources and why others were discarded, RCT and recent data preferred, patient-level data "generally expected"; "Simulations should be performed to evaluate the chances of erroneous conclusions, including ... false positive conclusions, under various scenarios of prior-data conflict"; plan a maximum amount of borrowing, sensitivity analyses on the prior, and analyse the current data with no borrowing. — [FDA-hosted E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- Section 5.4 time-to-event: power depends on the number of events; interim analyses at target event counts; adaptations changing the event target can be done by longer follow-up or more participants. — [FDA-hosted E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)

#### FDA, Use of Bayesian Methodology in Clinical Trials of Drug and Biological Products, draft, January 2026 (docket FDA-2025-D-3217; FR notice 12 Jan 2026) [REG-draft], read
- Success criteria are a threshold pair (a, c) in Pr(d > a) > c, set one of three ways: calibration to type I error (for a = 0 superiority, "determining the appropriate choice of c ... becomes a computational problem only"; derived algebraically for simple conjugate cases and "approximated using clinical trial simulations" for complex adaptive ones); direct interpretation of the posterior; or decision-theoretic loss minimisation (loss for approving an ineffective drug or not approving an effective one, including safety). — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download), page [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/use-bayesian-methodology-clinical-trials-drug-and-biological-products)
- Operating characteristics when not calibrated to type I error: "Bayesian power" (probability of success averaged over a prior, citing Spiegelhalter et al. 2004), probability of a correct decision (like a PPV), expected bias/MSE/coverage; the **design prior** (from which parameter values are drawn in simulation, e.g. centred on the MCID, and including pessimistic priors) is distinct from the **analysis prior**. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)
- Prior-data conflict "should be explored in simulations at the study design stage by varying the size of the assumed treatment effect over a sufficiently broad range of scenarios ..., including no effect in the target population". Formal expert elicitation (O'Hagan et al. 2006) is optional but must be pre-specified and discussed with FDA; quantify prior influence with effective sample size. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)
- Section VIII documentation list: justification for borrowing and feasibility of alternatives; every external source used and excluded; synthesis model; discounting justification; prior-data-conflict handling "supported by simulation results"; ESS; planned prior sensitivity analyses; success criteria per decision point; for calibrated designs type I error and power "under a range of plausible effect sizes" plus bias/MSE/coverage; for uncalibrated designs Bayesian power etc.; "a comprehensive simulation report" with code and "pre-specified, comprehensive, and plausible" scenarios; for MCMC, warmup, iterations, chains, convergence diagnostics. No fixed replicate number is stated in the draft text I searched (grep for "100,000"/"10,000" found none). — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)

#### Other procedure-level guides
- Granholm et al., "Designing and evaluating advanced adaptive randomised clinical trials: a practical guide" [PP; Danish ICU/biostatistics group; version 2025-01-15; full text read], arXiv: workflow = research question; interventions and common control; outcome type and generation; analysis timing and outcome-data lag; allocation rules; analysis model; adaptation (stopping, arm dropping) rules; **scenarios** (a null scenario based on the most likely reference outcome distribution plus at least two scenarios with differences, small ~ minimally relevant, large ~ largest realistic effect); performance metrics; **calibration** of stopping thresholds under the primary null; **sensitivity analyses** split into fixed design parameters (recalibrate) versus assumed parameters (reference event probability, inclusion rate; "stopping rules should be identical ... not recalibrated"); reporting of all design characteristics, assumptions, metrics, and earlier design iterations "even if not used", plus code, seeds, software versions. "Conducting 100,000 simulations is generally recommended when evaluating type 1 error rates, but fewer (e.g., 10,000) ... for other metrics." Worked example: 10,000 simulations per run, 5,000 bootstrap resamples for CIs. — [arXiv 2501.08765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08765)
- Kopp-Schneider, Wiesenfarth, Held, Calderazzo, "Simulating and reporting frequentist operating characteristics of clinical trials that borrow external information" [PP; abstract-only]: report the type I error of the test with borrowing, then calibrate the test without borrowing to that type I error so the power comparison is fair. — [arXiv 2302.12651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12651)
- Berry-type practice, one example only: a statistical analysis plan states FACTS software run over 18 response-rate scenarios with operating characteristics averaged over 10,000 simulated trials; FACTS 7.0 generates a design report for platform trials. [secondary; vendor documents] — [SAP NCT02749071](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/71/NCT02749071/SAP_001.pdf); [FACTS 7.0](https://www.berryconsultants.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Whats-New-in-FACTS-7.0.pdf)
- Replicate counts in a recent large neutral comparison: Klinglmuller et al. (Statistics in Medicine 2025) ran 2,500 runs per scenario over 10,608 scenarios and reports simulation SEs in tables; type I error pooled over 12,500 runs. [PR; partly read] — [PMC11840476](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11840476/)
- Zhao et al. validated analytic power with 1 million simulated trials per test per scenario. [PP; Merck authors] — [arXiv 2312.01723v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01723v2)

### Inferences
- A product "simulation report" that mirrors E20 section 5.2 items 1-11 would also satisfy FDA 2019 VIII.B, the FDA 2026 Bayesian report list and the CDE draft Appendix 2; the CDE structure adds only the explicit "recommendation" section.
- Defaults implied by the texts: nsim >= 100,000 per scenario for any reported type-I-error / false-positive number; 10,000 acceptable for power and expected sample size; seeds differ by scenario; report MCSE (or CI) next to every simulated proportion; store software version, seed, code. At p = 0.025: 1,000 runs give a 95% half-width of about 0.0097, 10,000 about 0.0031, 100,000 about 0.00097 (own arithmetic).
- Morris's own review shows the usual failure is reporting a count of 500-1000 without justification or MCSE; a tool that computes and prints MCSE and the n_sim rationale automatically addresses the commonest defect.
- The mandatory benchmark design (E20) means the tool should always run a non-adaptive or group-sequential comparator beside any proposed design.

## Q2. Where do the assumptions come from: control rate, effect size, dropout, accrual, design priors, elicitation, historical trials?

### Takeaway
Regulators accept literature, previous trials, extrapolation and structured expert input provided each source is documented and its uncertainty quantified; DELTA2 gives the most explicit taxonomy (important versus realistic difference, seven methods, standardised effect size as last resort). Industry practice (a 2022 survey) is dominated by "data from previous studies" (93-97%) with expert elicitation and publications at 70-80%; assurance is the main programme-level metric (89-94%).

### Cited Findings

#### Target difference: DELTA2 (Cook et al., BMJ 2018;363:k3750, also Trials 2018;19:606) [PR], read
- Seven methods in two groups. What is important: anchor, distribution, health economic, standardised effect size; what is realistic: pilot study, review of the evidence base; either: opinion seeking. Recommendations: where importance needs research, favour anchor and opinion-seeking, "do not use the distribution method", treat standardised effect size (Cohen 0.2/0.5/0.8) as "a last resort"; for realism use opinion seeking and review of the evidence base (ideally systematic reviews/meta-analyses of RCTs); pilot trials "typically too small to inform what would be a realistic difference" but can inform nuisance parameters (SD, control proportion). — [PMC6216070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6216070/)
- Choice of outcome should not be "solely on which gives the smallest sample size"; the target difference should be important to at least one stakeholder group but need not equal the minimum important difference if a larger difference is realistic or needed to change practice. — [PMC6216070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6216070/)
- Reporting items (Table 1, superiority trials): primary outcome; significance level and power; target difference (binary: absolute and/or relative difference plus both proportions; continuous: mean difference, common SD, standardised effect; time-to-event: absolute/relative difference, control event proportion, follow-up length, survival distributions, accrual time); allocation ratio; sample size and formula (number of events for survival); adjustments for loss to follow-up and multiplicity; "sensitivity of the sample size to its inputs"; plus, for protocols and grants, the basis for the target difference (important or realistic) and the method used. Scope excludes equivalence/non-inferiority, multi-arm, complex and Bayesian designs (per the 2017 protocol). — [PMC6216070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6216070/); scope note [PMC5469157](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469157/)

#### Regulatory statements on sources of assumptions
- E20: assumptions from "data from previous trials, publications, results from extrapolations, and expert input"; "all relevant sources of information available to the sponsor should be used"; quantify uncertainty and potential biases. FDA 2019 and CID: nuisance parameters (variance, control response probability, accrual speed) and "likely control group outcomes" are what the FDA wants to discuss at End-of-Phase-2. — [E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download); [FDA 2019](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download); [CID](https://www.fda.gov/media/130897/download)
- CDE draft external-data rules: named source, data-quality assessment before building the prior, clinical/design/time relevance, no selection of only favourable sources; randomised, controlled, double-blind confirmatory registration studies are the strongest source. — [draft reprint](https://www.xfdyb.com/show-2348.html)

#### Probability of success, assurance, design priors
- O'Hagan, Stevens, Campbell 2005: assurance = unconditional probability of a positive trial = prior expectation of power over the prior for the true effect; power conditional on one assumed effect "does not give a good indication"; sample size can be chosen to hit a target assurance; worked for normal, binary and gamma data. [PR; abstract-only] — [O'Hagan page](https://tonyohagan.co.uk/academic/abs/assurance.html)
- Kunzmann et al. 2021 (Am Stat 75(4):424-432) review "hybrid" Bayesian-frequentist sample size: assurance, probability of success, expected power are used inconsistently; they give consistent definitions. [PR; abstract-only] — [PMC7612172](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7612172)
- FDA 2026 draft: Bayesian power uses a design prior; "Design priors that explore pessimistic assumptions about treatment effect should also be considered"; differences in operating characteristics across design priors quantify sensitivity to the prior. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)
- Discounting phase 2 effects when planning phase 3: Kirby, Burke, Chuang-Stein, Sin, Pharm Stat 2012;11(5):373-385 (additive/retention-factor adjustment of the phase 2 estimate; used in the drugdevelopR package). [PR; citation and package vignette only] — [drugdevelopR vignette](https://packages.oit.ncsu.edu/cran/web/packages/drugdevelopR/vignettes/Bias_adjustment.html)

#### Expert elicitation
- GSK (Dallow, Best, Montague; company-authored): formal prior elicitation with the SHELF framework combined with assurance to inform milestone decisions; elicited beliefs give investment boards a probability of trial success and interim decision rules; a bimodal ("works or not, then effect size if it works") elicitation is discussed against over-optimism. [PP posted 2017 (the journal version is Pharm Stat 2018 per search results); abstract-only; the bimodal-elicitation detail comes from a search-result summary of the same PDF] — [arXiv 1708.09823](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.09823)
- Worked industry example (Crisp et al. 2018 as summarised in the EFSPI/PSI QDM paper): cardiovascular outcomes trial, priors elicited from individual experts and averaged into a consensus prior; adding an interim futility analysis plus a sample-size increase raised probability of success from 31% to 40%, with 80% conditional probability of success after passing the interim. [PP; secondary account of a PR paper] — [arXiv 2203.00684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.00684)
- FDA 2026 draft allows formal expert elicitation (O'Hagan et al. 2006) for the degree of borrowing, but only if pre-specified and agreed with the Agency. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)

#### How practice actually derives assumptions (survey)
- EFSPI/PSI quantitative-decision-making group questionnaire, 101 responses (54 statisticians, 47 non-statisticians; 13 for the portfolio level): clinical assumptions come from previous-study data (93-97%); internal and external historical data, publications and expert elicitation each ~70-80%; only ~20-30% say all trial teams in the company use the same method; barriers: data availability 45-54%, tools/software 31% of statisticians; programme-level decisions use assurance (89-94%) and power (50-62%); about two thirds say pre-specified decision rules are followed. Small, self-selected industry sample. [PP] — [arXiv 2203.00684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.00684)

#### Historical-trial-based design tools (vendor material)
- Unlearn, TrialPioneer, blog dated 1 April 2026 [VEN]: three modules — Scout (precedent search over PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, drugs@FDA), Hindsight (explore harmonised clinical and real-world datasets to "validate assumptions about population characteristics, endpoint behavior, and benchmarks"), SimLab (compare scenarios across endpoints, eligibility, sample size, "reproducible" and "tied to their assumptions"). Claims: up to 15% power boost from digital twins in Alzheimer's; an oncology model trained on ~300,000 biopsies validated on BREAKWATER where "only five patients in available datasets matched the eligibility criteria". No mechanism, inputs or scoring method is disclosed. — [Unlearn blog](https://www.unlearn.ai/blog/we-listened-to-sponsors-heres-what-we-built)
- Unlearn authors, "Digital Twins as Synthetic Controls in Single-Arm Trials", arXiv 2605.12832v1, 12 May 2026 [PP; vendor-authored], read: power for a single-arm trial with a model-based synthetic control needs the conditional outcome variance kappa^2 = sigma0^2 (1 - rho0^2), where sigma0^2 and the outcome-model correlation rho0 are estimated from an external control population with similar eligibility, plus the effective control sample size gamma x n0; asymptotic variance kappa^2 (1/n1 + 1/n0_eff); with an oracle outcome model the required single-arm size is one quarter of a 1:1 RCT's; in two reanalyses (ALS, Huntington's) AIPW cut variance by 40-60% relative to 1:1 propensity matching; continuous outcomes only, binary/time-to-event "require adaptation". — [arXiv 2605.12832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12832)

#### Time-to-event design inputs
- Zhao et al. [PP; Merck authors; partly read] model the trial with piecewise-constant enrolment, dropout and event rates and piecewise hazard ratios; worked example: enrolment 500/12 per month over 12 months, control median 15 months, dropout 0.001, HR 1 for 4 months then 0.6, 90% power, one-sided alpha 0.025, four analyses at months 12/20/28/36; result N = 643.5 and 426.4 final events. — [arXiv 2312.01723v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01723v2)

### Inferences
- The minimal honest input record per assumption is: value, role (important vs realistic), source type (DELTA2 method or "elicited" or "historical trial X"), uncertainty range, and whether it enters as a point (scenario) or distribution (design prior). DELTA2 and FDA 2026 both separate "what we want to detect" from "what we expect".
- A tool that lets a user type a single effect size with no provenance reproduces the pattern that DELTA2 calls out as a last resort (standardised effect size) and that the audits in Q7 find most often wrong.
- "Precedent-to-benchmark-to-simulation" (Unlearn) is only visible as marketing copy plus a technical preprint on single-arm continuous outcomes; the underlying benchmark comparison method is not published in what I found.
- Phase 2 estimates carry selection optimism; the Kirby-style discount is the only published remedy I found, and it was not read in full.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed description of Unlearn's "precedent to benchmark to simulation" workflow was found; the blog does not state inputs.
- SHELF method details (roulette, quartile, tertile) not retrieved; only the GSK application abstract and FDA citation of O'Hagan 2006.
- Kirby 2012, Crisp 2018 and Dallow 2018 full texts not read.
- Evidence on how often elicited priors beat historical-data priors for calibration: not found.

## Q3. Which inputs matter most, non-proportional hazards, dropout and accrual

### Takeaway
Effect size dominates sample size quadratically (halve the difference, four times the sample for a continuous outcome; for survival events scale with 1/ln(HR)^2); under a delayed or crossing effect the choice of test matters as much as N: in one published design setting the log-rank power falls from 0.875 to 0.804, 0.724 and 0.691 for no delay, 3-month delay, 6-month delay and crossing hazards while MaxCombo stays at 0.867, 0.848, 0.825 and 0.859. Dropout and accrual matter mainly through events and duration, and observed retention in publicly funded UK trials is usually better than the 10-20% allowance.

### Cited Findings
- DELTA2: sample size is "very sensitive to the target difference"; halving the target quadruples the sample size (two-arm, 1:1, continuous); "It is prudent to undertake sensitivity calculations" for misspecified control response rate or variance; sensitivity analyses on target difference and control proportion are a key recommendation. — [PMC6216070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6216070/)
- Aberegg et al. (critical care mortality trials): "inflation of delta has a substantially larger impact on required sample size than changes in the other variables". [PR; abstract-level via search summary] — [PMC2887200](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887200)
- Own arithmetic (two-sided alpha 0.05, 1:1, normal-approximation formulae; not from a paper; check in rpact/gsDesign before quoting):
  - Binary outcome, control 30%, power 80%, n per arm for relative risk reduction 20/25/33/40%: 859 / 540 / 294 / 198; at control 20%: 1,447 / 906 / 491 / 329; at control 40%: 564 / 356 / 196 / 133.
  - Same absolute difference of 10 points, control 20/30/40/50%: 199 / 294 / 356 / 388 per arm; at control 30%, absolute difference 10 / 8 / 5 points: 294 / 471 / 1,251; power 80% vs 90% at 10 points: 294 vs 392.
  - Continuous: SD underestimated by 20% means 1.44x the sample; by 50% means 2.25x.
  - Survival (Schoenfeld events, 1:1): events needed at 90% power for HR 0.60 / 0.70 / 0.80 / 0.90 = 162 / 331 / 845 / 3,787 (80% power: 121 / 247 / 631 / 2,829).
  - Dropout inflation 1/(1-d): 10% gives 1.11x, 20% gives 1.25x, 30% gives 1.43x (does not cover informative dropout).
  - Optimism illustration: trials expected HR 0.66 and observed 0.72 on average (see Q7); a trial sized for 90% power with 243 events at HR 0.66 has about 73% power at true HR 0.72 and would need 390 events (own arithmetic).
- Non-proportional hazards, design example [PP; Merck authors; Zhao, Zhang, Leon, Anderson, arXiv 2312.01723v2, partly read]: with N = 698, 3-year study, asymptotic power (the 1-million-run simulated power agrees within about 0.005): no delay: log-rank 0.875, FH(0,0.5) 0.842, MaxCombo 0.867, modestly weighted MWLR(12) 0.868, RMST(24) 0.820; 3-month delay: 0.804 / 0.867 / 0.848 / 0.851 / 0.637; 6-month delay: 0.724 / 0.854 / 0.825 / 0.828 / 0.450; crossing: 0.691 / 0.887 / 0.859 / 0.829 / 0.382. Under the "strong null" (early harm then benefit, survival equalising) type I error was 0.016-0.018 for log-rank but 0.042 for FH(0,0.5), 0.033 for MaxCombo, 0.205 for a zero-early-weight test, 0.025 for MWLR(12) and 0.011 for RMST. Workflow: specify piecewise rates; for log-rank compute events first then enrolment ("d-n"); for weighted tests and MaxCombo compute sample size from boundary-crossing probabilities ("n-d"); alpha/beta spending; implemented in gsDesign2 and simtrial. — [arXiv 2312.01723v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01723v2)
- Cross-pharma working group "straw man" guidance (Roychoudhury, Anderson, Ye, Mukhopadhyay; Stat Biopharm Res 2021; arXiv v4 12 Jan 2021): proposes a combination (MaxCombo) test for robust power across NPH alternatives; explicitly "not endorsed by regulatory authorities". [PP/PR; abstract-only] — [arXiv 1908.07112](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07112)
- Neutral comparison (Klinglmuller et al., Stat Med 2025;44(5):e70019, doi 10.1002/sim.70019) [PR; partly read]: 20 analysis variants over 10,608 scenarios in four classes (delayed onset 1,296; crossing 1,824; biomarker subgroups 5,760; progression 1,728), 2,500 runs each; FH(0,1) gave most power with long delay but was worst with no delay; MaxCombo "robust"; modestly weighted tests "a viable choice"; in the full study "the unweighted log-rank test was among the most powerful approaches" in most scenarios; authors report "a trade-off between interpretability and power". — [PMC11840476](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11840476/)
- Lakatos (Biometrics 1988;44:229-241): Markov-model sample size for the log-rank statistic allowing time-varying hazard ratios, dropout and non-compliance. [secondary description] — [NCSS PASS documentation](https://www.ncss.com/wp-content/themes/ncss/pdf/Procedures/PASS/Logrank_Tests_with_Non-Proportional_Hazards-Lakatos_and_Wu.pdf)
- Time-to-event adaptive designs: power depends on events, so interim looks are planned at event counts; adding information on not-yet-evented participants can inflate type I error. — [E20 5.4](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- Dropout and enrolment as simulated scenario factors: E20 5.2 lists "varying dropout or enrollment rates" explicitly; Granholm recommends sensitivity analysis of inclusion rates and reference event probabilities with unchanged stopping rules. — [E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download); [arXiv 2501.08765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08765)
- Accrual model: Anisimov and Fedorov, Statistics in Medicine 2007;26:4958-4975 — centre-level Poisson processes with gamma-distributed rates, parameters estimated by maximum likelihood, remaining recruitment time and intervals predicted by simulation, with an adaptive adjustment of centre numbers. [PR; secondary description, paper not read] — [JMP documentation of the implementation](https://www.jmp.com/support/downloads/JMPC70_documentation/OP_C_PR_0001.html)
- Recruitment-model survey: Gkioni et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2019;115:141-149: 13 eligible articles, 11 stochastic, one deterministic, one both; deterministic models are easy but "likely unrealistic", stochastic models need more parameters. A follow-up survey (J Clin Epidemiol 2020;124:58-68) is titled "Statistical models to predict recruitment in clinical trials were rarely used by statisticians in UK and European networks". [PR; abstract/title-level] — [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3052800/)
- Observed recruitment and retention against targets: NIHR HTA/MRC-funded UK trials recruiting 2002-2008: 55% reached the target, 78% reached 80% of target, 45% received an extension (73 trials) [PR; abstract-level] — [Sully 2013, PMC3691846](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3691846/). HTA-funded RCTs 2004-2016 (151 trials): 56% (85/151) reached target, 79% reached more than 80%; median recruitment 0.92 participants per centre per month (IQR 0.43-2.79); median retention 89% (IQR 79-97%), which the authors read as showing current attrition inflation of sample sizes "is reasonable". [PR] — [Walters 2017, BMJ Open 7:e015276](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/3/e015276)

### Inferences
- Ranking of influence for a product to make visible (from the formulae and the audits): (1) target effect (quadratic; and optimistic in practice), (2) control event rate/median or SD (nuisance; moves the absolute effect and the event count), (3) analysis method versus true effect shape (NPH), (4) power/alpha convention, (5) dropout (near-linear), (6) accrual (changes duration and event timing more than N).
- For survival the product should carry the hazard shape (delay, crossing) as an explicit scenario, not only a constant HR; the log-rank-only default is fragile in the published examples, but the 2025 neutral comparison also shows log-rank often competitive, so no single test is a safe default.
- Simulation of accrual needs a centre-level heterogeneity model (Poisson-gamma), not a constant rate; evidence that the UK median centre rate is under 1 participant per month with wide IQR supports modelling heterogeneity.

### Gaps
- No sensitivity study found that ranks all inputs head-to-head for binary, continuous and survival endpoints; the ranking above rests on formulae and separate audits. 
- Lin et al. 2020 (Stat Biopharm Res, cross-pharma comparison of NPH analysis methods) was not read; only its existence and arXiv location (1909.09467) were seen.
- Anisimov and Fedorov 2007 and Lakatos 1988 were not read in the original; accuracy of Poisson-gamma forecasts against real trials not found.

## Q4. Analytic-first versus simulation-first; group sequential, Simon two-stage, single-arm

### Takeaway
The regulators' default is analytic where theory exists (fixed designs, classical group sequential, Simon exact enumeration) and simulation when distributions are intractable or inference is Bayesian with borrowing; E20 still wants a benchmark design with well-understood properties inside any simulation. Analytic NPH group-sequential power has been shown to agree with a million-run simulation to about 0.005, so simulation is a verification step there, not the primary route.

### Cited Findings
- FDA 2019: traditional non-adaptive trials rely on theory for type I error and power; "For many adaptive designs, such as traditional group sequential designs, it is similarly possible to derive asymptotic probability distributions"; simulation is needed for "more complex adaptive designs", e.g. several adaptations or predictive-probability timing. — [FDA 2019](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- CID: simulation rather than formulae is "a common feature". — [CID](https://www.fda.gov/media/130897/download)
- FDA 2026: calibration constant c derived "algebraically" for simple conjugate/noninformative designs; simulation for complex ones. — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)
- E20: the simulation study "should include a benchmark design ... such as a non-adaptive or group sequential design"; for bias of treatment estimates "In some cases, bias and variability can be calculated analytically. In other cases, the evaluation has to rely on simulations." — [E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- Group sequential NPH: asymptotic vs 1-million-run simulated power differ by at most 0.005 in the table (e.g. 0.875 vs 0.876; crossing 0.691 vs 0.686) and the type I error under strong null is reproduced (0.018 vs 0.016); design workflow is analytic with simulation as check. — [arXiv 2312.01723v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01723v2)
- Simon (Controlled Clinical Trials 1989;10:1-10): two-stage single-arm phase II designs that minimise expected sample size under the null (optimal) or the maximum sample size (minimax) subject to type I/II error constraints, computed by exact binomial enumeration; Jung, Lee, Kim, George (Stat Med 2004;23(4):561-569): "admissible" designs under a Bayesian decision-theoretic criterion that contain Simon's minimax and optimal designs as special cases. [PR; abstracts/summaries] — [Simon, PubMed 2702835](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2702835/); admissible-design software listing [clinfun::twostage.admissible](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/clinfun/versions/1.1.5/topics/twostage.admissible)
- Single-arm with external control: required inputs and the analytic power formula are in the Unlearn preprint above (external-control variance, correlation of the outcome model, effective control sample size, ratio to an RCT; limit 1/4 for an oracle model). — [arXiv 2605.12832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12832)
- Borrowing designs: type I error and power by simulation, with the no-borrowing test calibrated to the borrowing test's type I error for a fair power comparison. — [arXiv 2302.12651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12651)
- Bayesian adaptive platform-style designs are simulation-first with calibration of stopping thresholds under the null, then sensitivity. — [arXiv 2501.08765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08765)
- Response-adaptive randomisation is flagged by E20 as susceptible to bias and type I error inflation under time trends (a case where simulation must include drift). — [E20 4.5](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)

### Inferences
- Decision rule for a product router: analytic for fixed-sample, classical GSD, exact single-arm; analytic plus simulation check for NPH weighted tests and MaxCombo; simulation-first for borrowing with dynamic discounting, sample-size re-estimation with unblinded data, response-adaptive randomisation, treatment/population selection, multi-endpoint with correlation, and any design where the user changes the decision rule from a standard one.
- The analytic/simulation split is also an audit tool: running both and flagging disagreement above Monte Carlo error is how the NPH paper validated its design.

### Gaps
- No source found that gives a quantitative threshold at which analytic approximations become unreliable (small samples, rare events); E20 only says simulation is used "with a specific sample size when analytical properties ... rely on large sample sizes".
- Simon 1989 and Jung 2004 full texts not opened.

## Q5. Comparing designs and presenting recommendations to decision makers

### Takeaway
Published guidance asks for a side-by-side comparison against a benchmark with explicit trade-offs, example trial traces and a plain clinical discussion; decision-theoretic framing (loss functions, value of information, go/no-go zones) exists in the literature and in the FDA 2026 draft but I found few published examples of the actual presentation tables.

### Cited Findings
- E20 report items 8-11: overall results with interactive graphs encouraged, single simulated example trials with different adaptations, limitations from uncertain assumptions, and "a clinical discussion about if and to what extent the simulation results address the key questions"; evaluation of "advantages and limitations of all design options ... to understand the tradeoffs". — [E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- FDA 2019: "it is good practice to evaluate the important operating characteristics of the proposed design as compared to alternative adaptive and non-adaptive designs ... However, the ultimate choice of design is the sponsor's responsibility"; example trials illustrate a positive result at the original size, a futility stop, and a positive result after size increase. — [FDA 2019](https://www.fda.gov/media/78495/download)
- CDE draft Appendix 2 closes with 总结与建议 (summary and recommendations). — [draft reprint](https://www.xfdyb.com/show-2348.html)
- FDA 2026: success criteria can be decision-theoretic ("minimize the expected loss", loss function may include safety and benefit-risk). — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)
- Granholm: report earlier design iterations "even if not used" to make the final choice transparent; sensitivity tables for design variants and for assumed parameters, with unchanged stopping rules. — [arXiv 2501.08765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08765)
- Industry decision framing: Crisp et al. example shows decision makers the overall probability of success (31% to 40%), probability of Go at interim and conditional probability of success after Go (80%). [PP secondary] — [arXiv 2203.00684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.00684). Worked phase II example of a decision-zone design (Frewer et al. 2016 as summarised in the same paper): target product profile with a base case of median PFS 10 to 14.6 months (HR 0.68, "target value" TV) and a downside case of 10 to 12 months (HR 0.83, "lower reference value" LRV); decision zones on the observed HR: Go if < 0.72, Consider between 0.72 and 0.86, Stop if >= 0.86; error rates are defined relative to TV and LRV rather than to the null (if true HR = TV, probability of Go or Consider = 90%; if true HR = LRV, probability of Go at most 20%; probability of landing in Consider at most 30%); the Consider zone gave "a considerably smaller sample size" than a Go/NoGo-only design at the price of lower probabilities of a Go for the base-case drug and of a NoGo for an insufficient drug, and the team judged the residual risks acceptable. — [arXiv 2203.00684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.00684)
- Lalonde/Frewer LRV-TV framework (Frewer, Mitchell, Watkins, Matcham, Pharm Stat 2016;15(3):255-263): stop if evidence is below LRV, go if above TV; example protocols state a false-stop risk of 10% and a false-go risk of 20% (one protocol with TV -3.0 and LRV -1.3 on its endpoint). [PR; protocol-level and slide-level evidence for the numbers] — [Cytel summary](https://cytel.com/perspectives/decision-making-in-early-clinical-development/); [protocol/SAP NCT04577794](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/94/NCT04577794/SAP_001.pdf); [Frewer slides](https://www.efspi.org/Documents/Events/Events%202018/Decision_Making_Dec_18/Presentations/4_Frewer_Paul.pdf)
- Value of information: Willan and Pinto (Stat Med 2005) pose sample size as maximising expected net gain (utility minus cost) instead of fixed alpha and power; Willan and Kowgier (Clin Trials 2008) show multi-stage designs can raise expected net gain and lower expected sample size; industry variant ties value to probability of approval and profit. [PR; abstract-level via search] — [McMaster listing](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/900456); [Willan and Kowgier, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18697843/)
- Admissible two-stage designs (Jung 2004) present a Pareto-style family between optimal and minimax. — [clinfun listing](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/clinfun/versions/1.1.5/topics/twostage.admissible)

### Inferences
- A trade-off table that regulators and sponsors already recognise: rows = designs (benchmark first), columns = type I error (with MCSE), power by scenario, expected and maximum sample size, expected duration, bias of the effect estimate, probability of early stop; plus 2-3 example trial traces and a one-paragraph clinical reading.
- Probability of success (assurance) must be shown next to conditional power and labelled by the design prior used; the Crisp example shows how an interim futility rule can be sold on assurance gain, not on power.
- If the product recommends one design, it should say whether the recommendation rests on a loss/utility function (and whose) or on constraint satisfaction.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published, openly available full example of a sponsor-facing design trade-off deck or a DSMB-facing simulation report; the only concrete artefacts are SAP-level scenario/replicate statements.
- Frewer 2016 and Willan 2005 full texts not read; the false-stop/false-go values come from protocols and slides.

## Q6. Trial outcome prediction claims (HINT, TrialBench, QuantHealth and others): what they do and how credible

### Takeaway
Published outcome-prediction models classify a coarse label (p < 0.05 on the primary outcome, or phase approval) with ROC-AUC roughly 0.60-0.77 on random or engineered splits; they do not estimate a design's operating characteristics. Vendor accuracy claims of 85-90% have no methodology or independent validation I could find.

### Cited Findings
- HINT (Fu et al.; arXiv 2102.04252 v1, Feb 2021; later in Patterns 2022) [PP version read; journal version not read]: label = success if the primary-outcome p-value < 0.05 on ClinicalTrials.gov results; restricted to small-molecule interventional trials with reported statistics; time-based split (earlier trials train, later test; split dates between March and August 2014); test sets were built to match FDA-guideline success ratios of 70%, 33% and 30% (phases I, II, III), so class balance is engineered. Reported (tables) HINT PR-AUC / F1 / ROC-AUC: Phase I 0.764 / 0.837 / 0.817; Phase II 0.602 / 0.638 / 0.761; Phase III 0.618 / 0.683 / 0.726; Phase III XGBoost reached ROC-AUC 0.719; text and tables of the same page disagree slightly (abstract values 0.772, 0.607, 0.623). Only binary primary-outcome success; devices and surgery out of scope. — [arXiv 2102.04252 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.04252). A search summary of the Patterns version gives F1 0.665/0.620/0.847 on 627/1,653/1,140 test trials (not verified in the full text) — [DeepAI record](https://deepai.org/publication/hint-hierarchical-interaction-network-for-trial-outcome-prediction-leveraging-web-data)
- TrialBench (Chen et al., Scientific Data 2025, doi 10.1038/s41597-025-05680-8) [PR; partly read]: 8 tasks (duration, dropout, serious adverse events, mortality, approval, failure reason, eligibility-criteria generation, dose finding); approval labels from TrialTrove (following HINT) plus negative samples from ClinicalTrials.gov "why stopped" text; approval baseline (single multimodal network, random 80/20 split) ROC-AUC Phase I 0.7649, II 0.7358, III 0.7298, IV 0.6052; precision/recall e.g. Phase III 0.7332 / 0.6211; failure-reason and dose labels produced by ChatGPT; temporal splits suggested but not reported; no limitation text on label noise or leakage. — [PMC12475113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12475113/)
- LLM versus HINT (Jin et al., arXiv 2411.17595 v2, Mar 2025) [PP; abstract-only]: GPT-4o best among LLMs but "all LLMs struggle to identify negative outcomes"; HINT better at negatives and more consistent across phases but underperforms in oncology; LLMs better on early phases and simple endpoints such as overall survival. — [arXiv 2411.17595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17595)
- QuantHealth (Katina) [VEN; press]: claims of simulating hundreds of thousands of protocol combinations, 85-90% "accuracy", ">100 simulated trials" at 85%, 86% on binary endpoints, later ">600 trials across 30 indications ... up to 90%"; trained on real-world data from 350 million patients; no definition of accuracy beyond a binary-endpoint metric and no independent validation found. (Which number belongs to which page was not checked individually.) — [Fierce Biotech](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/cro/quanthealth-debuts-new-ai-based-program-speed-clinical-trials); [Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20231130215509/en); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/quanthealth-launches-katina/)
- Base rate context: probability of success from phase 1 to approval for industry drugs 13.8% overall and 20.9% excluding oncology (about 186,000 trials, 2000-2015, Wong, Siah, Lo, Biostatistics). [PR; summary via secondary pages] — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/128480); [Allucent summary](https://www.allucent.com/resources/blog/why-do-clinical-trials-fail)
- The Unlearn BREAKWATER validation (vendor blog) is a single illustrative comparison, with no numbers beyond "five patients matched". — [Unlearn blog](https://www.unlearn.ai/blog/we-listened-to-sponsors-heres-what-we-built)

### Inferences
- These models answer "will a registered trial report p < 0.05", conditional on registry features; a design simulation needs the distribution of the outcome under specified effect, variance, dropout and accrual. At most the models can feed a design prior for assurance, with a very wide spread (AUC 0.6-0.77 on engineered splits).
- Random splits (TrialBench) leak near-duplicate trials (same drug/sponsor families); only HINT uses a time split, and its test class balance is set by design. I did not find a leakage audit.
- A "predicted success probability" shown to a user next to simulated power would invite the false reading that two different quantities agree.

### Gaps
- QuantHealth: no preprint or paper found; accuracy definitions unknown.
- Patterns version of HINT and the TrialBench full tables beyond approval not read; no independent external validation of either found.
- A search summary attributed "trial status prediction balanced accuracy 50-55%" to a 2025 LLM trial-design benchmark; I could not verify which paper, so it is not used.

## Q7. Practical sample-size reporting errors and wrongly assumed inputs, with figures

### Takeaway
Audits find sample-size calculations incompletely reported (about half omit required parameters), often not reproducible (30% of reproducible ones differ by more than 10%), and built on effect sizes that are larger than later observed in most fields: critical-care trials predicted 10.1% absolute mortality reduction and observed 1.4%; oncology phase 3 trials expected HR 0.66 and observed 0.72. Control-rate assumptions err in both directions with large relative errors in about a third of trials.

### Cited Findings
- Charles, Giraudeau, Dechartres, Baron, Ravaud, BMJ 2009;338:b1732 [PR; read]: 215 two-arm parallel superiority RCTs in six high-impact general journals, 2005-2006. No calculation reported in 10 (5%); required parameters missing in 92 (43%); all reported in 113 (53%). Alpha not reported in 24, power in 15, control group assumption in 50, treatment effect assumption in 29; assumptions were justified in 81 (49%) of those giving a control assumption and 50 (27%) of those giving an effect assumption. Of 164 replicable, 157 compared with the reported target: equal in 27 (17%), within 5% in 76 (48%), differed by more than 10% in 47 (30%) and by more than 50% in 10 (6%) (28 with replicated size at least 10% below the reported, 19 with replicated size larger). Control-group assumption versus observed in 145 trials: median relative difference 3.3% (IQR -16.7 to 21.4), absolute relative difference above 30% in 45 (31%) and above 50% in 24 (17%), assumed values higher or lower than observed "in roughly even proportions". Median assumed treatment effect 33.3% for binary/time-to-event outcomes and standardised effect 0.53 for continuous outcomes. Only 73 (34%) were "adequate" (enough data to replicate, within 10%, control assumption within 30%). Of 96 trials with a registered expected size, 46 (48%) matched the reported target. — [PMC2680945](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2680945)
- Aberegg, Richards, O'Brien, Crit Care 2010;14:R77 ("delta inflation") [PR; abstract-level]: 38 critical-care mortality RCTs, mean predicted delta 10.1% versus observed 1.4% (delta gap 8.7%, P < 0.0001); observed exceeded predicted in 2/38 (5.3%); significant in the hypothesised direction in 7/38 (18.4%); delta justified in 5/38 (13.2%), baseline mortality justified in 7/38 (18.4%). — [PMC2887200](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887200)
- Nontshe et al., Southern African Journal of Anaesthesia and Analgesia 2018;24(2) [PR; page read]: 28 anaesthesia RCTs; relative difference between expected and actual event rates greater than 20% in 80% of trials and greater than 50% in 44%. — [SAJAA](https://sajaa.co.za/index.php/sajaa/article/view/1099)
- Seehra et al., Eur J Orthod 2021 [PR; page read]: 85 systematic reviews, 347 trials, 110 (31.7%) described a sample-size calculation; assumed effects back-calculated for 73; assumed greater than observed (one-sided signed-rank P < 0.001). — [PMC8488969](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8488969)
- Oncology: J Natl Cancer Inst 2025, doi 10.1093/jnci/djaf161, "Expected versus observed effect sizes for survival endpoints in phase 3 oncology trials" [PR; known only via a French cancer-institute bulletin summary]: 111 phase 3 trials in ten top journals (2023); average expected HR 0.66 versus observed 0.72 (signed-rank p = .0035); of 143 observed HRs for primary survival endpoints 82 (57.3%) were weaker than expected, 59/84 (70.2%) in the five lower-impact-factor journals. — [INCa bulletin](https://en-www.cancer.fr/professionnels-de-sante/veille/nota-bene-cancer/bulletin-n-651-du-7-juillet-2025/expected-versus-observed-effect-sizes-for-survival-endpoints-in-phase-3-oncology-trials)
- Recruitment: 55% reached target (Sully 2013) and 56% (85/151, Walters 2017); median retention 89% (IQR 79-97%) so the standard 10-20% attrition allowance was judged reasonable. — [PMC3691846](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3691846/); [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/3/e015276)
- Mechanism named in the literature: "delta inflation" — start from the sample that can be recruited and adjust the assumed effect until it fits (described in the orthodontic and anaesthesia audits' discussion; the original characterisation is Aberegg 2010). — [PMC8488969](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8488969); [PMC2887200](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887200)

### Inferences
- Most frequently wrong, in order of documented size: (1) effect size too large (all fields audited), (2) event/control rate with large relative error in a third of trials but no systematic direction in the one general audit, (3) recruitment too fast (about 45% miss target), (4) attrition allowance (usually adequate on average), (5) the shape of the effect over time (NPH, see Q3).
- Reporting defects (missing alpha/power/control/effect assumptions, no justification for 51% of control and 73% of effect assumptions in the 2005-06 sample) are exactly what DELTA2 Table 1 and ACE item 7a target; a structured input form that cannot be saved with a blank item would remove the largest class of defects.
- An "assumption audit" feature can benchmark a user's effect size against published realised-versus-planned ratios (e.g. planned HR 0.66 realising as 0.72) and show the power loss it implies (about 73% instead of 90% in my arithmetic).

### Gaps
- Charles 2009 is a 2005-06 sample; a post-2015 replication with similar metrics was not found (a search summary mentioned a 2025 meta-epidemiological study of ethics committees rarely challenging effect-size assumptions, 6% of comments, not opened).
- A search summary also attributed to Zakeri et al. (NCI cooperative-group trials) a median observed-to-proposed HR ratio of 1.26 over 81 trials 2007-2017, and a figure of 13 of 54 trials failing a proportional-hazards test; neither source page was opened, so neither is used as a finding.
- Frequency of misestimated dropout (versus assumed) beyond the HTA retention median: not found.

## Cross-cutting: what the published workflows imply for the inputs a virtual-trial product must collect (synthesis of the sections above)

### Takeaway
Across ADEMP, FDA, ICH E20, FDA 2026 Bayesian, CDE and DELTA2 the workflow has the same eight stages, and the "real inputs" are a short list the user must supply or justify; computation (nsim, seeds, MCSE, calibration, analytic cross-check) can be automated.

### Cited Findings
- E20 5.2 gives the eight-stage skeleton explicitly (objectives, candidate designs plus benchmark, operating characteristics, scenarios and their justification, implementation, results, limitations, clinical discussion); see Q1. — [E20](https://www.fda.gov/media/188961/download)
- DELTA2 Table 1 and FDA/CDE documentation lists fix the minimal recorded inputs; see Q2 and Q1. — [PMC6216070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6216070/); [FDA 2026](https://www.fda.gov/media/190505/download)

### Inferences (my synthesis; not a published procedure)
Stage map (decision question to report), with what each source asks for:

| Stage | What the literature asks for | Input the user must give | Can the platform automate it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Decision question | E20 objectives "directly related to the decisions"; ADEMP Aims; CID asks for purpose and erroneous-conclusion risk | Phase/purpose (go/no-go vs confirmatory), estimand, primary endpoint, comparator | No (needs the person); can template |
| 2 Design candidates | E20 benchmark + alternatives; FDA rationale versus alternatives; Granholm design choices | Look number/timing, adaptation types, allocation, analysis test (incl. NPH choice) | Generate benchmark and standard alternatives automatically |
| 3 Data-generating assumptions | Effect (important vs realistic), nuisance (control rate/SD/median), operational (accrual, dropout, follow-up, lag), external data and prior if borrowing | Values with source and uncertainty; external datasets with relevance judgement | Pull candidate benchmarks from registries/historical trials; flag values outside historical range |
| 4 Analysis | Pre-specified methods per estimand; Bayesian success criteria (a, c) or alpha | Test/model, success threshold, multiplicity | Yes, from catalogue |
| 5 Operating characteristics | Type I error over a null grid, power over effect grid, expected/max N, duration, bias, coverage, example trials | Grid ranges (or accept defaults tied to step 3 uncertainty) | Yes (analytic first, simulation if needed; nsim >= 100,000 for type I error cells) |
| 6 Sensitivity | DELTA2 sensitivity to inputs; E20 prior and scenario robustness; Granholm no-recalibration rule; CDE critical-weight analysis; prior-data-conflict scenarios | Which assumptions are uncertain (ranges) | Yes (one-at-a-time and grid; tornado-style ranking) |
| 7 Recommendation | E20 trade-offs and clinical discussion; FDA decision-theoretic option; industry PoS/Go-NoGo | Loss/utility or acceptable risk levels (e.g. false stop 10%/false go 20% style) | Draft; person confirms |
| 8 Report | E20 11 items; FDA VIII.B; CDE Appendix 2; ACE 7a/24b | Free text for clinical discussion and limitations | Assemble; include seeds, code, software, MCSE |

- Minimum non-automatable input set: estimand/endpoint; target effect with its role and source; control-arm parameter(s) with a plausible range; operational assumptions (accrual, dropout); decision rule/alpha; external data relevance if borrowing; the decision stakes.
- Everything else in the report is computable by the platform: replicate counts and their rationale, seeds, MCSE, benchmark design, example trials, scenario grid defaults, the analytic-versus-simulation cross-check.

### Gaps
- None of the sources specifies a validated procedure for "assumption audit against historical trials" as a regulated step; the benchmark idea is from Unlearn marketing and the general E20 requirement to quantify uncertainty.
