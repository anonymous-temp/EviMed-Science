# Actionable research questions on missed haemodialysis sessions and adherence

**Direction as given.** Identify actionable questions about missed dialysis sessions and adherence.

**Population.** Adults receiving maintenance haemodialysis.

**Setting.** A community hospital.

**Data already held.** Attendance and transport records from that hospital. No biospecimens.

**Constraints.** Six months; no new recruitment; one part-time analyst.

**Search scope.** PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, Crossref, fifteen guideline documents, eleven trial-registry records, and open-web sources were searched between 2026-10-04 and 2026-10-04 for missed and shortened haemodialysis treatments, attendance measurement, transport and travel time to dialysis, geographic access, seasonal and day-of-week patterning, non-adherence determinants, adherence interventions, and routinely-collected-data and prediction-model reporting standards. Records were screened by title and, where available, abstract; open-access full texts were read for seven works.

---

## 1. Executive summary

The mortality association has been established often enough that another replication would add little. What the literature has not produced is agreement on how a missed session should be measured, transport treated as an exposure rather than as a reason code, and any study carried out at single-centre scale on the records a community hospital actually holds.

Three findings shape the agenda that follows.

1. **The same phenomenon is measured four incompatible ways, and the choice dominates every number that follows.** Published definitions of "missed" include percentage of scheduled sessions absent (1.2% per month in one US unit [1]; 2.4% of 2,297,227 treatments in a 15,340-patient network [2]), percentage of *patients* missing at least one session in a window (5.1–7.6% per four weeks [3]), a patient-level threshold (two or more missed sessions per month [4]; three or more in six months [5]), and percentage of prescribed therapy not delivered (6.6–7.9% of patients missed 10% or more [3]). A single centre that reports one of these and not the others cannot be compared with any of them.
2. **Shortened sessions outnumber missed ones by roughly four- to six-fold in the two samples that report both, and they are separately consequential — yet "adherence" in this literature almost always means absence.** In the same 28,108-treatment sample, 5.1–7.6% of patients missed a treatment per four weeks while 26.8–32.3% had at least one shortened treatment [3]; over twelve weeks, half of all patients had at least one missed or shortened treatment [3]. In 2,297,227 treatments, 2.4% were missed and 15.4% were shortened [2]. Among 9,543 kidney transplant recipients, shortened treatments predicted allograft loss (adjusted hazard ratio 1.54, 95% CI 1.12–2.13), mortality (1.34, 1.13–1.58) and acute rejection (adjusted odds ratio 3.88, 1.98–7.58), while missed treatments were associated only with mortality [6].
3. **Transport is the exposure the literature names but does not quantify.** Everywhere it appears, it is a reason code; nowhere in this set is it a measured variable. In one US unit, "lack of transportation later in the day" was recorded as the reason for 7.7% of 2,108 early sign-offs but is not reported for the 387 no-shows in the same unit [1]. Longer travel time predicted missed treatments in an international study of 4,493 patients [7], and the most recent integrative review reports private transport to the dialysis centre as a *protective* factor for adherence (odds ratio 1.82, 95% CI 1.05–3.15) while the same review's estimate for "difficulty coming to dialysis" as a predictor of absenteeism is null (odds ratio 1.41, 95% CI 0.96–2.07, p = 0.09) [8]. Those three statements cannot all be about the same mechanism.

The hospital's own records land on the third of those gaps and are sufficient for the first two. The recommended first study (§6) is a single retrospective study of the unit's own records with three pre-specified aims and no new recruitment.

## 3. Where the field disagrees

**3.1 Transport.** Three statements that cannot all be right sit side by side in the literature. Longer travel time predicts missed treatments in an international sample of 4,493 patients [7]. Private transport to the centre is protective in the most recent integrative review [8]. The direct estimate of "difficulty coming to dialysis" as a predictor of absenteeism in that same review is null, with a confidence interval crossing 1 (odds ratio 1.41, 95% CI 0.96–2.07, p = 0.09) [8]. The oldest source in the set puts transport in a third place entirely — as a reason for *stopping a session early* rather than for not arriving (7.7% of 2,108 early sign-offs) [1]. These are not competing estimates of one quantity; they are different exposures (distance, mode, self-reported difficulty) against different outcomes (missed, shortened, or a composite). No study in this set measured an institution's own transport records against its own session-level attendance.

**3.2 Which exposure matters more.** The field's attention is on missed sessions, but the two largest datasets each rank shortened sessions as the more frequent event — about six-fold in the larger one (15.4% versus 2.4% of treatments [2]) and roughly four- to six-fold in the other (26.8–32.3% versus 5.1–7.6% of patients per four weeks [3]); that comparison is this report's own reading of those two sets of figures, not a quantity either paper states, and the only study in this set that separated the two against hard outcomes found shortened treatments doing more of the work [6]. Whether that reflects a real difference in mechanism or simply that shortening is measured better is untested.

**3.3 Unit effects versus patient effects.** Four-month missed-treatment risk varied more than 50-fold across countries, from under 1% to 24% [7]. The determinants literature, meanwhile, is dominated by patient-level factors such as age, race, dialysis vintage and depression [2] [8]. A single-centre study cannot resolve the country-level question, but it can test whether within-unit variation is organised by schedule, shift, transport provider or weekday, which are unit-level levers.

**3.4 Direction of the race and vintage findings.** The integrative review reports Black patients at higher odds of non-adherence (odds ratio 3.98, 95% CI 1.42–11.22, p < 0.001) [8] and, in the same section, that patients on dialysis more than five years were less likely to adhere (p < 0.001) while another study in that review found longer dialysis experience associated with *better* adherence (odds ratio 0.992, 95% CI 0.986–0.998, p = 0.005) [8]. These are stated as a contradiction within one review, and the review does not resolve it.

## 4. Evidence gaps

1. **No study located applies multiple published definitions of a missed session to the same attendance dataset** and reports how much the resulting burden estimate moves. The heterogeneity is documented [8]; its magnitude on a single dataset is not. The definitions in use also draw on different data rather than differing only in a threshold — reason codes recorded at the time of an early sign-off [1], network billing records [2], a patient-level threshold for classification [4], and dates of service [38] — so the comparison Q1 proposes is between genuinely different measurements, not one measurement with several cut-points. Guidance requires the monitoring without supplying the metric: the 2006 adequacy guideline directs every centre to monitor and minimise missed and shortened treatments and records 4% of treatments per month missed among US patients [34], leaving the unit of measurement to the centre.
2. **No study located measures transport as an exposure from records rather than as a reason code or a self-report.** The closest works quantify travel distance at the population level [17] or access time from a spatial model [18] [19], not attendance at one unit.
3. **No located study tests the seasonality of missed sessions at unit level**, although the most recent single-centre cohort names it as untested while reporting seasonal variation in a downstream outcome [4], and inclement-weather work has already established weather as an exposure at national scale: among 60,135 patients at in-centre clinics across the north-eastern United States in 2001–2019, a time-stratified case-crossover analysis with conditional Poisson regression and a distributed lag model found an incidence rate ratio for a missed appointment of 1.03 per 10 mm of rainfall (95% CI 1.02–1.03) and 1.02 for snowfall (1.01–1.02) on the day of exposure, and over a seven-day window 1.55 (1.22–1.98) for hurricane and tropical-storm exposure, 1.29 (1.25–1.31) for sustained wind advisories and 1.34 (1.29–1.39) for wind-gust advisories [36]. What is missing is not the association but its local and seasonal structure in one unit's own records.
4. **No located study reports the joint distribution of missed and shortened sessions at patient level**, so the overlap between the two behaviours is unknown.
5. **No located prediction model for missed sessions uses transport features**, and no located study reports temporal validation of such a model in a single unit.
6. **Group-based trajectory modelling of adherence over time was searched for and not located** in haemodialysis; a trajectory-model query returned paediatric kidney-transplant rehabilitation adherence, which is off-target. The bounded scope of that negative result is stated in §7.
7. **Interventional evidence specific to attendance remains thin, and the recent attempts have not changed that**: one randomised cross-over proof-of-concept with a 1:36 cost-benefit ratio [21], a 2024 randomised peer-education trial whose between-group effect was on fluid restriction and not on attendance [39], syntheses whose most recent dialysis-specific meta-analysis component is dated [22] [23] [24], and a therapeutic alliance literature of 3 studies and 130 patients [25]. No located trial reports a between-group attendance effect measured from session records: the trials that report attendance gains report them as questionnaire sub-scores [40], and the peer-education trial, which measured attendance behaviour directly, found no between-group difference [39]. None of the located designs treats *rescheduling* as an outcome, although the payment-model evaluation shows re-capture moving independently of attendance [38].

## 5. Candidate questions

Each candidate states its PICO, design, required data fields, comparator evidence, feasibility verdict against the stated constraints, and novelty. **Fields that the hospital is not known to hold are flagged as `MISSING — required`.**

### Q1. How much does the measured burden of missed and shortened sessions move when published definitions are applied to the same attendance records?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital. I: not applicable (measurement comparison). C: each published definition set against the others on the same denominator. O: prevalence of non-attendance and of shortened treatment, per definition, with exact binomial confidence intervals.

**Design.** Retrospective measurement study on existing attendance records. No new recruitment, no biospecimens.

**Required data fields.** Session-level: scheduled date; scheduled shift and day of week; attended / not attended; if attended, delivered minutes; prescribed session duration; prescribed sessions per week; patient identifier. Patient-level: date of dialysis initiation, age, sex, primary renal diagnosis, vascular access type, transplantation or transfer or death date (for censoring), and any documented reason for non-attendance.

`MISSING — required`: prescribed session duration and delivered minutes. If either is absent, the shortened-session definitions in [2] and [3] cannot be applied and the study reduces to missed sessions only — a material loss, because shortened sessions are the more frequent event [2] [3] and the one that separated outcomes in transplant recipients [6].

**Comparator evidence.** Reported prevalence: 1.2% of sessions absent per month in one unit of 231 patients over 31,212 sessions [1]; 2.4% of 2,297,227 treatments missed and 15.4% shortened in a 15,340-patient US network [2]; 5.1–7.6% of 860 patients missing at least one treatment per four weeks and 6.6–7.9% missing 10% or more of prescribed therapy [3]; 19.8% of 360 incident patients classified poorly adherent under a threshold of two or more missed sessions per month [4]; 1.6% of sessions missed with 23.7% of 202 patients missing three or more sessions in six months [5]. The definitions in that set differ in the data they read as well as in their arithmetic: a payment-model evaluation identified skipped treatments from dates of service, as days on which a patient was neither hospitalised nor received an expected treatment [38].

**Feasibility. Feasible.** Purely a re-derivation on held records. One part-time analyst over six months is sufficient; the analysis is descriptive and the only computational requirement is correct handling of the prescribed-sessions denominator.

**Novelty:** No direct answer identified in this search. The heterogeneity of definitions is documented [8], but no located study reports the spread of estimates that results from applying competing definitions to one dataset. Closest neighbours are the integrative review's instrument-inconsistency section [8] and the multi-definition prevalence reports themselves [1] [2] [3] [4] [5], none of which compares definitions within one dataset.

**Falsification.** The claim "definition choice materially changes the estimated burden" is falsified if all applicable definitions yield prevalences within 2 percentage points of each other in this unit.

### Q2. Is non-attendance in this unit organised by weekday, shift, season or dialysis frequency rather than by patient characteristics?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital. I: not applicable (exposure patterning). C: weekday and shift categories against each other; seasons against each other; session-frequency groups against each other. O: rate of missed sessions and rate of shortened sessions per scheduled session, by stratum, with interval estimates.

**Design.** Retrospective cohort of all scheduled sessions in the record window, with the session as the unit of analysis and a patient-level random effect to account for repeated sessions. No new recruitment.

**Required data fields.** All fields in Q1, plus: calendar date for seasonal assignment; documented unit seasonal definitions (`MISSING — required`: the season or month windows must be pre-specified and recorded, since the seasons used in the only comparable single-centre study were defined a priori as November–March, April–May, June–August, September–October [4]).

**Comparator evidence.** Saturday predominance of missed and shortened treatments, and higher odds of missing at least one treatment per month on a Tuesday–Thursday–Saturday versus Monday–Wednesday–Friday schedule (odds ratio 1.33, p < 0.0001), in 15,340 patients [2]. Better attendance on Sunday, Tuesday and Thursday than on Saturday, Monday and Wednesday (odds ratio 1.65, 95% CI 1.03–2.64, p < 0.05) in the most recent review's pooled estimate [8]. Poor adherence rising from 5.3% at one session weekly to 41.4% above three sessions weekly (p = 0.004) in 360 incident patients [4]. Night-shift scheduling associated with worse adherence than day shift (p = 0.042) [8].

**Feasibility. Feasible.** The only added burden over Q1 is stratification and a repeated-measures model; both are within one part-time analyst's capacity in six months. The seasonal element is only interpretable if the record window covers at least two full years — otherwise power is inadequate and seasonality should be dropped rather than reported.

**Novelty:** Occupied, but not in this population and setting. The weekday and shift associations are established in large US and international samples [1] [2] [7] [8], so a single-unit replication is confirmatory rather than novel. The seasonality question is the genuinely open part: no located study reports missed or shortened sessions by season, and the only recent single-centre cohort in this set states explicitly that it did not examine whether missed treatments varied by season [4]. The exact axis of difference is therefore *outcome* (session attendance rather than emergency department visits) and *setting* (one community hospital rather than a provincial cohort).

**Falsification.** The seasonality hypothesis is falsified if the seasonal rate ratio for missed sessions has a confidence interval containing 1 across all pre-specified season contrasts with adequate power, or if the number of seasons with usable data is fewer than three.

### Q3. Do the hospital's own transport records explain missed and shortened sessions, and do they explain shortening rather than absence?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital who use any arranged or reimbursed transport. I: transport exposure — mode (own vehicle, family or friend, public, hospital-arranged, ambulance or non-emergency medical transport), provider, one-way distance or travel time, whether a voucher or reimbursement was used, and the time of day of the scheduled return trip. C: patients and sessions with lower versus higher transport exposure. O: missed session; shortened session, analysed separately.

**Design.** Retrospective cohort with the session as the unit and patient-level random effects; transport exposures pre-specified before data extraction. Confounder set pre-specified: age, dialysis vintage, session frequency, shift, vascular access, documented comorbidity count, distance decile.

**Required data fields.** All fields in Q1, plus transport: mode per session or per period; provider identifier; one-way distance or travel time; voucher or reimbursement flag and date; scheduled pickup time relative to session end.

`MISSING — required`: the linkage key and its resolution. Transport records and attendance records must share a patient identifier and a date or period key that allows session-level or at least period-level join. If transport is recorded only at patient level or at an aggregated monthly level, session-level transport attribution is not possible and the design must fall back to a period-level analysis, which weakens the causal story considerably.

**Comparator evidence.** Lack of transportation later in the day as the recorded reason for 7.7% of 2,108 early sign-offs in one US unit of 231 patients, with transport *not* reported as a reason category for the 387 no-shows in the same unit [1]. Longer travel time predicting missed treatments among 4,493 patients [7]. Private transport to the centre associated with adherence (odds ratio 1.82, 95% CI 1.05–3.15, p < 0.05) in the most recent integrative review [8], against a null estimate for difficulty coming to dialysis predicting absenteeism (odds ratio 1.41, 95% CI 0.96–2.07, p = 0.09) in the same review [8]. Mean one-way driving distance of 7.9 miles overall and 15.9 miles rural versus 6.2 miles urban, with longer travel associated with increased mortality and decreased quality of life [17].

**Feasibility. Reduced scope.** Feasible only if the linkage key resolves to the session or at least to the period, and only if transport exposure varies within patients over time. If transport is effectively constant per patient, the analysis is a cross-sectional comparison of patient groups with no within-person contrast, and its confounding by indication is severe — patients who need hospital-arranged transport differ systematically from those who do not. In that case the honest output is a description of the transport-usage distribution plus a stated inability to separate transport from the patient characteristics that determine transport eligibility.

**Novelty:** *No direct answer identified in this search, with bounded scope.* The three positions in §3.1 are all that this search located: transport as a reason code for early sign-off [1], travel time as a predictor in an international cohort [7], and self-reported transport as a correlate in a review of 29 studies [8]. A search of the hospital's own transport records against its own session-level attendance was not located in any indexed study. Bounded scope: the negative result rests on the retrieval described in §7; a study using institutional transport records may exist in administrative or quality-improvement literature that is not indexed in the sources searched, and this has not been excluded.

**Falsification.** The transport hypothesis is falsified if, after adjustment for the pre-specified confounders, the transport exposure coefficient for missed sessions and for shortened sessions both have confidence intervals containing 1, or if within-patient transport variation over the record window is too small to estimate a within-person effect.

### Q4. Can next-month missed and shortened sessions be predicted from attendance and transport history, and does the model beat a one-variable rule?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital. I: prediction model using prior attendance, transport exposure and basic clinical covariates. C: a rule that predicts non-attendance in the next month from whether the patient missed any session in the current month. O: discrimination and calibration for non-attendance in the following 30 days, with a temporal split.

**Design.** Retrospective prediction-model development with a temporal split: earlier period for development, later period for validation. Reported to TRIPOD+AI [37] for the model and RECORD [10] for the routinely-collected data. No new recruitment.

**Required data fields.** All fields in Q1 and Q3. `MISSING — required`: an event count adequate for the number of candidate predictors. With one part-time analyst and a single unit, the candidate predictor set must be fixed in advance at a small number — the transport contrast, prior-month non-attendance, shift, session frequency and vintage — and no automated selection may be run.

**Comparator evidence.** No located prediction model for missed haemodialysis sessions uses transport features; the predictors reported in this set are study-level associations in 4,493 patients (younger age, less dialysis vintage, shorter treatment time, lower Kt/V, longer travel time, more depressive symptoms) [7] and patient-level odds in 15,340 patients (age under 55, odds ratio 1.33; African American, 1.51; Native American, 1.50; Hispanic, 1.33; all p < 0.0001) [2]. The only predictive instrument identified is the EpxDialysis trial's poor-attendance definition of missing 2–6 sessions in 12 weeks, which was a trial entry criterion rather than a validated prediction model [21].

**Feasibility. Reduced scope.** The full prediction-model agenda is not deliverable by one part-time analyst in six months on one unit's records, because developing and temporally validating a model with a usable calibration plot requires more events than a single community hospital is likely to accumulate, and because the reporting standard implies pre-specified predictors, a calibration assessment and a model-updating path [37]. The reduced-scope version is a pre-specified logistic model with at most five predictors and a temporal validation split, reported with confidence intervals and an explicit statement that the calibration estimate is imprecise. If the event count in the development period is below the threshold set before analysis, the study reports descriptive associations only and does not fit a model.

**Novelty:** No direct answer identified in this search. No located study builds or temporally validates a predictor of next-month missed or shortened sessions in a single unit using transport records. Closest neighbours are the DOPPS predictor analysis [7], which is a study-level association analysis in a multinational cohort, and the EpxDialysis trial's attendance threshold [21], which is an entry criterion. Neither is a validated unit-level prediction model.

**Falsification.** The claim that a model adds information beyond the single-variable rule is falsified if the difference in discrimination between the model and the prior-month-non-attendance rule has a confidence interval containing zero on the temporal validation set.

### Q5. Did the unit's transport or reimbursement arrangement change attendance when it changed?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital. I: a documented change in transport provision, voucher policy, or dialysis scheduling. C: the period before the change versus after. O: missed and shortened session rates per scheduled session.

**Design.** Interrupted time series or before-and-after analysis on attendance records, with the intervention date specified in advance from administrative documentation rather than inferred from the data.

**Required data fields.** All fields in Q1 and Q3, plus the administrative record of the change and its date.

`MISSING — required`: documentation of an actual change with a date. Without it there is no exposure and no study.

**Comparator evidence.** The only randomised evidence located on attendance specifically is the automated-messaging trial, a cross-over design against poor attendance defined as missing 2–6 sessions in 12 weeks at Washington University-affiliated centres, reporting a median 75% improvement in appointment adherence, a 31% reduction in unintended hospitalisation days and a cost-benefit ratio of 1:36 [21]. Registered studies of peer mentorship, motivational interviewing, technology-assisted collaborative care and virtual reality are complete, active or recruiting but have not reported results in this set [26] [28] [29] [30] [31].

**Feasibility. Not feasible unless a change is documented.** With no new recruitment and no prospective intervention, this design exists only if the administrative record already contains a discrete change. Even then, six months for one part-time analyst is enough only for a simple pre-post comparison with a pre-specified primary rate difference; a full interrupted time series with seasonality terms requires more time points than a single unit typically provides.

**Novelty:** Not applicable as a novelty question — the answer is local and the design is established. It is included because the value lies in the local estimate, not in the method. If no change is documented, this candidate is dropped.

**Falsification.** A before-after difference is uninterpretable, and therefore the finding is falsified as a causal statement, if the pre-change and post-change periods differ in patient composition beyond what the record captures — for example if a shift change or a population shift coincides with the transport change.

### Q6. Does local non-attendance reproduce the published mortality and hospitalisation association?

**PICO.** P: adults on maintenance haemodialysis at one community hospital. I: missed or shortened sessions. C: patients with and without non-attendance. O: all-cause hospitalisation, and all-cause mortality if the record supports it.

**Design.** Retrospective cohort with survival analysis on the session-derived exposure.

**Required data fields.** All fields in Q1, plus outcome data: hospital admissions with dates, and death dates with a source. `MISSING — required`: whether the hospital holds outcome data at all, whether admissions occurring at other hospitals are captured, and whether death ascertainment is complete.

**Comparator evidence.** All-cause mortality hazard ratio 1.68 (95% CI 1.37–2.05) per one or more missed treatments in the four months before enrolment among 4,493 patients, with cardiovascular mortality, sudden death, hospitalisation, and laboratory deviations also associated [7]. Relative mortality risks of 1.17 to 2.18 for average treatment duration under 3.5 hours versus over 3.5 hours in 600 patients [13]. Relative risk ratio 7.34 (95% CI 2.81–19.20) for more than three emergency department visits among non-adherent patients in 360 incident patients [4].

**Feasibility. Not feasible as a primary aim; reduced scope as a secondary analysis.** A single community hospital over a limited record window will accumulate too few deaths to estimate a mortality hazard with useful precision, and if admissions at other hospitals are not captured the outcome is systematically under-ascertained. The defensible version is a secondary descriptive analysis reporting the observed event count and the width of the interval actually achieved, explicitly declining to test the published hazard ratio rather than reporting an underpowered estimate as a replication.

**Novelty:** Answered. The association between missed treatments and mortality has been established in 4,493 patients internationally [7], in 600 patients for treatment duration [13], and for shortened treatments against transplant outcomes in 9,543 recipients [6]. Re-deriving it in one unit adds no new knowledge. This candidate is listed so that it is not mistaken for a gap, and it is not pursued as a primary aim.

## 6. Prioritization and recommended first study

**Ranking.** Q1 and Q2 rank first, run as one study: they are the only candidates that are unambiguously feasible with what the unit already holds, they ask the least of the analyst, and Q1's result conditions how every other number is read — a unit that does not know its own definitional spread cannot be compared with the literature into which it is trying to insert itself. Q3 ranks third and is the intellectually strongest candidate, because it targets a documented contradiction in the field (§3.1) and uses data the hospital uniquely holds; it ranks below Q1 because its feasibility is contingent on a linkage key that has not been confirmed. Q4 ranks fourth as a reduced-scope secondary aim of the same study. Q5 is contingent on documentation. Q6 is dropped as a primary aim for the reason stated.

**Rationale, stated explicitly.** Priority here is not novelty alone. Q6 is the most cited association in the field and scores lowest, because an established association re-derived at single-centre scale produces an imprecise estimate of a known quantity. Q1 has almost no novelty as a method and scores highest, because the measurement choice governs whether any number the unit produces means anything to anyone else. Novelty enters the ranking only at Q3, and it does so because the contradiction it addresses is unresolved rather than merely unreplicated.

**Recommended first study: a retrospective study of the unit's own attendance and transport records with three pre-specified aims.**

*Aim 1 (primary).* Estimate the burden of missed and shortened sessions under each applicable published definition, with exact binomial confidence intervals, and report the spread across definitions.

*Aim 2.* Describe the distribution of missed and shortened sessions by weekday, shift, season and prescribed session frequency, using a pre-specified season definition, with the session as the unit and patient-level random effects.

*Aim 3 (secondary, contingent).* Estimate the association between recorded transport exposure and missed sessions, and separately between transport exposure and shortened sessions, adjusted for the pre-specified confounder set.

**Pre-specified primary definition of a missed session.** A scheduled session with no attendance recorded within the scheduled session window, counted against the number of sessions *prescribed* for that patient in that period rather than against sessions attended plus missed. The denominator choice is pre-specified because it is the single largest source of divergence among the published definitions [1] [2] [3] [4] [5], and it is recorded before extraction so that it cannot be selected after the counts are seen.

**Minimum data fields.** As listed in Q1, with the shortened-session fields (prescribed duration, delivered minutes) and the transport linkage key marked as mandatory gates: if prescribed duration or delivered minutes are unavailable, Aim 1 reports missed sessions only and states the omission; if the transport linkage key does not resolve to the session or period, Aim 3 is not performed and the reason is reported.

**Analysis plan.** Descriptive estimates with exact binomial intervals. Poisson or negative binomial regression with patient-level random effects for the patterning in Aim 2, using rate per scheduled session as the outcome. For Aim 3, the same model with transport exposure added, fitted separately for missed and shortened sessions, with no stepwise selection and the model specification fixed before extraction. Sensitivity analyses pre-specified: the alternative denominators in Aim 1; exclusion of sessions adjacent to a hospital admission; and, for Aim 3, restriction to patients with within-person transport variation.

**Falsification criterion.** Each aim is falsifiable independently: Aim 1's hypothesis that definition choice materially changes the estimate fails if all applicable definitions land within 2 percentage points; Aim 2's patterning hypothesis fails if the weekday and shift rate ratios have confidence intervals containing 1; Aim 3's transport hypothesis fails if the adjusted transport coefficients for both missed and shortened sessions have confidence intervals containing 1.

**Governance.** Ethics review and any data-sharing or transport-provider information-governance approvals are requirements of this study, not assumptions that they are already in place; extraction should not begin until they are documented.

## 7. Limits of this assessment

Every novelty claim above rests on the retrieval described in §1 and reaches no further than that retrieval. Searches of PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, Crossref, the guideline channel (fifteen guideline documents, of which the 2015 and 2006 KDOQI haemodialysis adequacy guidelines were read in full) and the trial-registry channel (eleven registered studies), plus open-web sources, were run; two Europe PMC queries on attendance patterning by season and day of week, and on transport voucher or rideshare interventions, returned no usable records; two open-access retrievals failed and the affected works are cited at abstract level only; one preprint-source search was rejected by the source because it requires an exact identifier rather than a text query, so **preprints were not systematically searched** and a relevant preprint may exist. Where a candidate is marked "no direct answer identified", read that as "not found in this search", not as established absence. Screening was by title and abstract, with open-access full texts read for seven works and full guideline text read for the two adequacy guidelines; every work is labelled in the evidence map by the level at which it was actually read, and no design or effect claim rests on a title.

No biospecimen or genomic data are involved, and no individual treatment recommendation follows from this report.
